Theorem. There exist an absolute constant $\delta > 0$ and an infinite sequence $\{P_i\}$ of finite point sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $|P_i| \to \infty$ and $u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}$ for all $i$, where $u(P_i) = \frac{1}{2} | \{ (p,q) \in P_i \times P_i : \|p-q\|_2 = 1 \} |$ is the number of unit-distance pairs.

Remark. This result disproves the Erdős Unit Distance Conjecture.

Assistant

I will write \(\mathbb R^2\) as \(\mathbb C\). A useful common reduction is the following.

If \(A\subset \mathbb C\) is finite and \(U\subset S^1=\{z:|z|=1\}\) is a set of unit translations, then

\[ E_U(A):=\bigl|\{(a,u)\in A\times U:a+u\in A\}\bigr| \]

gives unit-distance pairs in \(A\). More precisely,

\[ u(A)\ge \frac12 E_U(A), \]

because an unoriented edge can be counted at most twice, once in each direction. Thus a very natural strategy is:

Build finite sets \(A_i\subset\mathbb C\) and many unit translations \(U_i\subset S^1\) such that \(A_i\) has small boundary under all translations in \(U_i\), so that

\[ E_{U_i}(A_i)\approx |A_i||U_i|, \]

and arrange \(|U_i|\ge |A_i|^\delta\) for some fixed \(\delta>0\).

The classical Erdős lattice construction does this with \(A\) a square grid and \(U\) the lattice points on one circle, but in \(\mathbb Z[i]\) the number of such directions is only \(|A|^{o(1)}\). The approaches below are different ways of overcoming exactly that bottleneck.

Approach 1 — High-degree number fields and relative \(S\)-units

Core idea: use many small prime ideals to get exponentially many unit directions

This is the approach I would pursue first. It looks closest to a complete proof.

The obstruction in the Gaussian-integer construction is that if all directions have a common denominator \(N\), then the number of rational unit directions is controlled by a divisor function of \(N\), hence is subpolynomial in \(N\). The way around this is to move to number fields of growing degree where a fixed rational prime \(p\) splits into many prime ideals of norm \(p\). Then the “divisor function” becomes exponential in the degree.

The basic arithmetic gadget is the following.

1.1. Choose a totally real field with many split primes

Fix a rational prime

\[ p\equiv 1\pmod 4. \]

We would like an infinite sequence of totally real fields \(F_i\) with

\[ d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty \]

such that \(p\) splits completely in \(F_i\). Thus

\[ p\mathcal O_{F_i}=\mathfrak P_{i,1}\cdots \mathfrak P_{i,d_i}, \]

with each \(\mathfrak P_{i,j}\) of norm \(p\).

Then set

\[ K_i=F_i(i). \]

Let \(\tau\) denote complex conjugation on \(K_i/F_i\). Since \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), \(p\) also splits in \(\mathbb Q(i)\), so each \(\mathfrak P_{i,j}\) splits further in \(K_i\):

\[ \mathfrak P_{i,j}\mathcal O_{K_i} = \mathfrak p_{i,j}\,\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}, \]

where \(\tau(\mathfrak p_{i,j})=\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}\), and

\[ N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(\mathfrak p_{i,j})=N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j})=p. \]

So in \(K_i\) we have \(d_i\) conjugate pairs of prime ideals of the same tiny norm \(p\).

The desired field sequence should also have controlled root discriminant or at least root discriminant growing slowly enough. A natural source is a totally real class-field tower or Golod–Shafarevich-type tower in which \(p\) splits completely. At the brainstorming stage, the important point is that such a tower would give us exponentially many small prime ideals while keeping geometry-of-numbers constants under control.

1.2. Use relative \(S\)-units of norm \(1\)

Let

\[ S_i=\{\mathfrak p_{i,1},\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,1},\dots, \mathfrak p_{i,d_i},\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,d_i}\}. \]

Consider the relative norm-one \(S_i\)-unit group

\[ \mathcal U_i^{-} = \{u\in \mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times} : u\tau(u)=1\}. \]

The condition \(u\tau(u)=1\) is the key. For any complex embedding \(\sigma:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\), because \(F_i\) is totally real and \(\tau\) is complex conjugation over \(F_i\), we have

\[ \sigma(\tau(u))=\overline{\sigma(u)}. \]

Thus

\[ u\tau(u)=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma(u)\overline{\sigma(u)}=1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\sigma(u)|=1. \]

So every such \(u\) gives an honest Euclidean unit vector under every complex embedding.

This is the crucial advantage over ordinary algebraic units. Integral relative norm-one units in \(K_i/F_i\) are essentially finite, by Kronecker-type phenomena, but \(S\)-units with allowed poles at the split primes are abundant.

By the \(S\)-unit theorem, the rank of \(\mathcal U_i^{-}\) should be \(d_i\). Indeed,

\[ \operatorname{rank}\mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times} = (d_i-1)+2d_i=3d_i-1, \]

because \(K_i\) has \(d_i\) complex places and \(|S_i|=2d_i\). Meanwhile,

\[ \operatorname{rank}\mathcal O_{F_i,S_i\cap F_i}^{\times} = (d_i-1)+d_i=2d_i-1. \]

Taking the relative norm-one kernel leaves rank

\[ (3d_i-1)-(2d_i-1)=d_i. \]

Thus \(\mathcal U_i^{-}\) contains exponentially many elements if we restrict the valuations at the primes in \(S_i\) to a bounded box.

For a fixed integer \(T\ge 1\), define a finite set of unit directions by

\[ U_i(T) = \left\{ u\in \mathcal U_i^{-}: |v_{\mathfrak p_{i,j}}(u)|\le T \text{ for all }j \right\}. \]

Since \(u\tau(u)=1\), we automatically have

\[ v_{\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}}(u) = -v_{\mathfrak p_{i,j}}(u). \]

One expects, after controlling the relevant class-group index, a lower bound of the form

\[ |U_i(T)|\ge C_U^{d_i} \]

for some \(C_U>1\), once \(T\) is chosen large enough but fixed independently of \(i\).

This is the arithmetic replacement for the Gaussian divisor count. In \(\mathbb Z[i]\), the number of available unit directions grows subpolynomially. Here, because \(p\) splits into \(d_i\) separate prime pairs of norm \(p\), the number of choices grows exponentially in \(d_i\).

1.3. Build a finite point set stable under those translations

Let

\[ \mathfrak a_i(T) = \prod_{\mathfrak q\in S_i}\mathfrak q^{-T}. \]

Then every \(u\in U_i(T)\) lies in \(\mathfrak a_i(T)\). Indeed, its valuation at every prime in \(S_i\) is at least \(-T\), and it has no poles outside \(S_i\).

Embed \(K_i\) into

\[ K_i\otimes_\mathbb Q\mathbb R \cong \mathbb C^{d_i} \]

using its \(d_i\) complex embeddings

\[ \iota_i(x)=(\sigma_{i,1}(x),\dots,\sigma_{i,d_i}(x)). \]

For a parameter \(R>1\), define an adelic/Minkowski box

\[ B_i(R)= \left\{ z=(z_1,\dots,z_{d_i})\in\mathbb C^{d_i}: |z_j|\le R\text{ for all }j \right\}. \]

Now define

\[ A_i=A_i(R,T) = \{x\in \mathfrak a_i(T):\iota_i(x)\in B_i(R)\}. \]

This is finite because we impose archimedean bounds in all embeddings.

Also define the inner set

\[ A_i^\circ=A_i(R-1,T) = \{x\in \mathfrak a_i(T):|\sigma_{i,j}(x)|\le R-1 \text{ for all }j\}. \]

If \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and \(u\in U_i(T)\), then

  1. \(x,u\in \mathfrak a_i(T)\), hence \(x+u\in \mathfrak a_i(T)\);

  2. for every embedding \(\sigma_{i,j}\),

    \[ |\sigma_{i,j}(x+u)| \le |\sigma_{i,j}(x)|+|\sigma_{i,j}(u)| \le (R-1)+1=R. \]

Therefore

\[ x+u\in A_i. \]

So every pair \((x,u)\in A_i^\circ\times U_i(T)\) gives an edge after projecting to one complex embedding.

Fix one embedding \(\sigma_i=\sigma_{i,1}\), and set

\[ P_i=\sigma_i(A_i)\subset\mathbb C\simeq\mathbb R^2. \]

The map \(\sigma_i\) is injective on \(K_i\), so

\[ |P_i|=|A_i|. \]

For \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and \(u\in U_i(T)\),

\[ |\sigma_i(x+u)-\sigma_i(x)| = |\sigma_i(u)| = 1. \]

Hence

\[ u(P_i)\ge \frac12 |A_i^\circ|\,|U_i(T)|. \]

1.4. Counting

The covolume of \(\iota_i(\mathfrak a_i(T))\) is essentially

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}\,N(\mathfrak a_i(T)). \]

Since

\[ N(\mathfrak a_i(T))=p^{-2Td_i}, \]

a standard geometry-of-numbers estimate should give

\[ |A_i(R,T)| \asymp \frac{(\pi R^2)^{d_i}p^{2Td_i}}{\sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}}, \]

up to factors exponential in \(d_i\) that can be absorbed into constants if the root discriminants are controlled.

Suppose, schematically, that we have constants \(C_N,C_I,C_U>0\), independent of \(i\), such that

\[ |A_i|\le C_N^{d_i}, \]
\[ |A_i^\circ|\ge C_I^{d_i}, \]

and

\[ |U_i(T)|\ge C_U^{d_i}. \]

Typically \(C_I\) is close to \(C_N\), with

\[ \frac{C_I}{C_N}\approx \left(\frac{R-1}{R}\right)^2. \]

Then

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 C_I^{d_i}C_U^{d_i}. \]

Since

\[ |P_i|=|A_i|\le C_N^{d_i}, \]

we get

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

provided

\[ C_I C_U > C_N^{1+\delta}. \]

Equivalently, because \(C_I\approx \beta C_N\) with

\[ \beta=\left(\frac{R-1}{R}\right)^2, \]

it suffices to have

\[ \beta C_U>1. \]

Then one may take

\[ \delta < \frac{\log(\beta C_U)}{\log C_N}. \]

The parameters \(T,R,p\) are fixed once and for all, so \(\delta>0\) is absolute.

1.5. Why this approach is promising

This approach directly attacks the main weakness of the classical construction.

In \(\mathbb Q(i)\), if one wants many rational unit vectors with a common denominator, the denominator has to contain many distinct rational primes, and the divisor function only gives \(N^{o(1)}\) directions. Here, by moving to fields \(K_i=F_i(i)\) where a fixed rational prime \(p\) splits into \(d_i\) prime pairs, one obtains \(d_i\) independent \(p\)-adic choices at bounded cost. That converts the subpolynomial divisor-count phenomenon into an exponential-in-degree phenomenon.

The technical lemmas needed are concrete:

  1. Find a tower \(F_i\) of totally real fields with \(p\) split completely and controlled root discriminant.
  2. Control the relative \(S\)-unit valuation lattice so that \(|U_i(T)|\ge C_U^{d_i}\).
  3. Prove uniform lattice-point estimates for the fractional ideals \(\mathfrak a_i(T)\).
  4. Choose \(T,R\) so that \(\beta C_U>1\).

This is the most promising approach because the unit-length condition is exact, not approximate, and the counting mechanism naturally gives a fixed positive exponent.

Approach 2 — Finite-field unit graphs plus arithmetic lifting

Core idea: start from dense finite-field unit-distance graphs and lift a positive-exponent substructure to \(\mathbb R^2\)

The finite-field analogue of the unit-distance problem has extremely dense examples. Over \(\mathbb F_q\), define

\[ G_q=\{(x,y)\in \mathbb F_q^2\}, \]

with an edge between \(a,b\) when

\[ (a_1-b_1)^2+(a_2-b_2)^2=1. \]

The finite-field unit circle

\[ S_q=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb F_q^2:x^2+y^2=1\} \]

has about \(q\) points, so the Cayley graph has

\[ n=q^2 \]

vertices and about

\[ q^3=n^{3/2} \]

edges.

Of course, one cannot simply identify \(\mathbb F_q^2\) with a subset of \(\mathbb R^2\). The key question is whether one can lift a sufficiently large, sufficiently structured piece of this finite-field graph into characteristic zero while preserving exact Euclidean unit distances.

2.1. Work over a split residue ring instead of a single finite field

The number-field construction from Approach 1 suggests a refined finite model.

Let \(K_i=F_i(i)\) as before, with \(p\) split completely. Let

\[ \mathfrak m_i(T) = \prod_{j=1}^{d_i} \mathfrak p_{i,j}^T\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}^T. \]

The residue ring

\[ R_i(T)=\mathcal O_{K_i}/\mathfrak m_i(T) \]

decomposes into a product of local factors. Since the primes over \(p\) have residue field \(\mathbb F_p\), this ring is morally like a product of \(2d_i\) copies of \(\mathbb Z/p^T\mathbb Z\).

Complex conjugation \(\tau\) acts by swapping each pair

\[ \mathfrak p_{i,j} \longleftrightarrow \overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}. \]

Inside \(R_i(T)^\times\), consider the local relative norm-one group

\[ H_i(T)=\{h\in R_i(T)^\times:h\tau(h)=1\}. \]

At each split pair this looks like

\[ (a,a^{-1}), \]

so the local norm-one group is large.

The finite Cayley graph on the additive group of \(R_i(T)\), with generating set \(H_i(T)\), is a natural finite-field prototype. It has many vertices and many norm-one translations.

2.2. Lift local norm-one elements to global norm-one elements

The desired lift would proceed as follows.

For many elements \(h\in H_i(T)\), use strong approximation or an explicit Chinese-remainder construction to find global elements

\[ u_h\in K_i^\times \]

such that

\[ u_h\tau(u_h)=1 \]

and

\[ u_h\equiv h\pmod{\mathfrak m_i(T)}. \]

Then for every complex embedding \(\sigma\),

\[ |\sigma(u_h)|=1. \]

Thus each \(u_h\) gives a genuine Euclidean unit translation after applying a chosen embedding \(\sigma_i:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\).

This lifting step is the crucial bridge between finite-field incidence and real Euclidean geometry.

2.3. Lift vertices using balanced representatives

The vertices of the finite graph live in the residue ring \(R_i(T)\). We need to choose representatives

\[ s:R_i(T)\to K_i \]

such that the images

\[ A_i=s(R_i(T)) \]

are controlled in all archimedean embeddings.

Ideally, one wants a balanced section satisfying something like

\[ |\sigma_{i,j}(s(r))|\le R \]

for all embeddings and all selected residues \(r\).

Then, for many pairs \((r,h)\), one wants

\[ s(r+h)=s(r)+u_h \]

as an equality in \(K_i\), not merely modulo \(\mathfrak m_i(T)\). This is a “no carry” condition. One way to enforce it is to restrict to an interior subset of representatives, exactly as one uses an inner box in Approach 1.

Projecting via \(\sigma_i\), one obtains

\[ P_i=\sigma_i(A_i)\subset\mathbb C. \]

Whenever the no-carry condition holds,

\[ |\sigma_i(s(r+h))-\sigma_i(s(r))| = |\sigma_i(u_h)| = 1. \]

So finite-field edges lift to real unit distances.

2.4. Why this approach is promising

This approach has a very compelling model: finite-field unit graphs already have far more than \(n^{1+\delta}\) edges. The challenge is to preserve a positive-exponent fraction of this density when lifting to characteristic zero.

The benefit of using the split number-field setting is that the local finite model and the global real embedding are linked by the same relative norm-one torus

\[ R^1_{K_i/F_i}\mathbb G_m. \]

That torus simultaneously gives:

  1. many finite residue classes of norm \(1\);
  2. global \(S\)-units with archimedean absolute value \(1\);
  3. additive boxes of representatives stable under many of those translations.

So Approach 2 can be viewed as a finite-field/CRT version of Approach 1.

The main technical issues are:

  • One cannot hope to lift the full finite-field graph at exponent \(3/2\), because the real planar incidence bound gives \(u(n)=O(n^{4/3})\). Therefore the construction must either thin the generator set or lose enough edges at the boundary.
  • The lifting must produce exact norm-one elements \(u_h\), not approximate ones.
  • The representative set must avoid carry/wraparound for many translations.
  • One must control collisions after projecting to a single complex embedding.

Still, this is a strong second approach because the finite-field object already has the desired combinatorial shape. The problem becomes one of preserving enough of that shape through an arithmetic lifting procedure.

Approach 3 — Incidence-geometric construction via small expansion by a circular arc

Core idea: build an exact Szemerédi–Trotter-type incidence configuration for congruent circles

Unit distances can be viewed as incidences between a point set and unit circles centered at the same point set. The Szemerédi–Trotter-type upper bound \(O(n^{4/3})\) for unit distances comes from incidence geometry. For arbitrary lines or pseudocircles, \(n^{4/3}\)-type constructions are classical. The difficulty is to realize a positive-power fraction of such an incidence pattern using congruent circles.

A very concrete way to formulate the problem is through curved sumsets.

3.1. Parameterize the unit circle

Use the rational parametrization

\[ \gamma(t) = \left( \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \frac{2t}{1+t^2} \right) \in S^1. \]

If \(A\subset\mathbb R^2\) and \(T\subset\mathbb R\), define

\[ B=A+\gamma(T) = \{a+\gamma(t):a\in A,\ t\in T\}. \]

Then every pair \((a,t)\in A\times T\) gives a unit-distance pair

\[ a \longleftrightarrow a+\gamma(t). \]

Thus, with

\[ P=A\cup B, \]

we have at least

\[ |A||T| \]

oriented unit-distance pairs, up to a harmless factor for duplicates.

Therefore, if one can arrange

\[ |B|=|A+\gamma(T)|\ll |A|^{1+\alpha} \]

while

\[ |T|\gg |A|^\beta \]

with

\[ \beta>\alpha, \]

then

\[ |P|\ll |A|^{1+\alpha}, \]

whereas

\[ u(P)\gg |A|^{1+\beta}. \]

This gives

\[ u(P)\gg |P|^{1+\delta} \]

with

\[ \delta=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{1+\alpha}>0. \]

So the geometric problem becomes:

Find a large finite set \(A\subset\mathbb R^2\) and a large finite set of unit translations \(\Gamma=\gamma(T)\subset S^1\) such that the curved sumset \(A+\Gamma\) is unusually small.

This is an additive-combinatorial reformulation of the theorem.

3.2. Why the usual grid fails

If \(\Gamma\) is a generic set on a strictly convex curve, then \(A+\Gamma\) tends to expand almost maximally.

For example, if \(A\) is itself a grid and \(\Gamma\) is an arbitrary large set of unit vectors, one expects

\[ |A+\Gamma|\approx |A||\Gamma| \]

unless \(\Gamma\) has strong arithmetic structure relative to \(A\).

The classical lattice construction chooses \(A\) to be a square grid and \(\Gamma\) to be the set of lattice points on one circle. But then \(|\Gamma|=|A|^{o(1)}\).

So this incidence approach must find a new source of structured unit translations.

3.3. Local circular-arc version

One can also localize the problem near a single direction, say near \((1,0)\). Points on a small arc of the unit circle can be written as

\[ \gamma_{\mathrm{loc}}(t) = (\sqrt{1-t^2},t) \]

for small \(t\). Then a unit edge between two nearby clusters has the form

\[ b=a+\gamma_{\mathrm{loc}}(t). \]

Equivalently,

\[ b_y-a_y=t, \]

and

\[ b_x-a_x=\sqrt{1-t^2}. \]

Expanding,

\[ \sqrt{1-t^2} = 1-\frac12t^2+O(t^4). \]

Thus locally the unit-distance relation resembles an incidence relation with translates of a parabola. This suggests trying to imitate the usual grid construction for point-line incidences, but replacing lines by carefully chosen translates of a circular arc or parabola.

A target local gadget would be:

  • two small disks \(D_0,D_1\) centered at points distance \(1\) apart;
  • finite sets \(A\subset D_0\), \(B\subset D_1\), each of size about \(m\);
  • at least \(m^{1+\eta}\) unit distances between \(A\) and \(B\).

Such a gadget alone would already prove the theorem. Even weaker gadgets might be amplified recursively by replacing vertices of a bounded-degree unit-distance graph with small clusters and using the gadget along each edge direction.

3.4. Algebraic way to seek small curved sumsets

The rational parametrization

\[ \gamma(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right) \]

identifies the unit circle with a one-dimensional algebraic torus. Indeed, in complex notation,

\[ \gamma(t) = \frac{1+it}{1-it}. \]

Thus one can try to choose \(T\) from a multiplicative grid in a number field and choose \(A\) from an additive grid that is stable under translations by \(\gamma(T)\).

This geometric approach therefore naturally leads back to norm-one tori and \(S\)-units, but it can be pursued in a more analytic/incidence-theoretic way:

  1. Choose \(T\) so that \(\Gamma=\gamma(T)\) has many algebraic relations.
  2. Build an additive set \(A\) with small expansion under \(\Gamma\).
  3. Use incidence bounds inversely: a large incidence configuration with congruent circles should force hidden algebraic-group structure.
  4. Exploit that structure to make the construction exact.

The main difficulty is proving a genuinely small curved-sumset estimate

\[ |A+\Gamma|\le |A|^{1+\alpha} \]

with \(|\Gamma|\ge |A|^\beta\) and \(\beta>\alpha\). For generic real sets this is false, so the construction must be highly arithmetic.

3.5. Why this approach is promising

This approach is geometrically natural. It attacks the theorem at the level of the unit-distance incidence relation itself, without initially committing to a particular number field.

It also connects to extremal incidence geometry: the known upper bound \(O(n^{4/3})\) suggests that a construction with exponent \(1+\delta\) should look like a partial Szemerédi–Trotter extremizer. The challenge is to realize such an extremizer using congruent circles rather than arbitrary lines or pseudocircles.

The weakness is that the exact algebraic construction of \(A\) and \(\Gamma\) is subtle. In practice, this path may rediscover the relative \(S\)-unit construction from Approach 1. But it is still valuable because it gives a clean additive-combinatorial target:

\[ \boxed{\text{Find many unit translations } \Gamma\subset S^1 \text{ and a finite }A\subset\mathbb C \text{ with } |A+\Gamma|\ll |A|\,|\Gamma|.} \]

Any solution to that target immediately yields many unit distances.

Ranking

1. Most promising: relative \(S\)-units in high-degree fields

This is the most concrete path. It directly supplies:

  • many exact unit translations \(u\) because \(u\tau(u)=1\);
  • finite point sets \(A_i\) stable under those translations;
  • exponential-in-degree counts for both points and directions;
  • a clear route to a fixed positive \(\delta\).

The main arithmetic ingredients — split primes, \(S\)-unit ranks, lattice-point counting, and root-discriminant control — are standard types of tools. This approach looks closest to a complete proof.

2. Second most promising: finite-field graphs plus arithmetic lifting

Finite-field unit graphs already have the desired density, so they provide an excellent blueprint. The challenge is transferring enough of that structure into characteristic zero without losing exact unit distances.

This approach is promising because it can be combined with the same norm-one torus machinery as Approach 1. However, the lifting/no-carry step is delicate, and one must thin the finite-field graph enough to respect real planar incidence constraints.

3. Third most promising: incidence-geometric curved-sumset construction

This is the most geometric and perhaps the most flexible conceptually. It reframes the problem as constructing a finite set \(A\) with small expansion under many translations from the unit circle.

It is attractive because any success gives the theorem immediately, and it aligns with the incidence-theoretic upper bound. But it is less immediately constructive than the \(S\)-unit strategy. The main difficulty is forcing exact algebraic overlap among translates of a strictly curved set.

Summary of the most likely proof route

I would start with Approach 1 and try to prove the following package of lemmas.

  1. Field tower lemma. There are totally real fields \(F_i\) of degrees \(d_i\to\infty\), with controlled root discriminant, in which a fixed prime \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) splits completely.

  2. Relative \(S\)-unit counting lemma. For \(K_i=F_i(i)\), with \(S_i\) the primes over \(p\), the group

    \[ \mathcal U_i^{-}=\{u\in\mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times}:u\tau(u)=1\} \]

    contains at least \(C_U^{d_i}\) elements with bounded \(S_i\)-valuation, for some fixed \(C_U>1\).

  3. Adelic box counting lemma. For the fractional ideals

    \[ \mathfrak a_i(T)=\prod_{\mathfrak q\in S_i}\mathfrak q^{-T}, \]

    the sets

    \[ A_i=\{x\in\mathfrak a_i(T):|\sigma(x)|\le R \text{ for every complex embedding }\sigma\} \]

    satisfy \(|A_i|\le C_N^{d_i}\) and have inner subsets \(A_i^\circ\) of size \(\ge C_I^{d_i}\).

  4. Parameter inequality. Choose fixed \(T,R\) so that

    \[ \frac{C_I}{C_N}C_U>1. \]

Then with

\[ P_i=\sigma_i(A_i)\subset\mathbb C, \]

one obtains

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 |A_i^\circ|\,|U_i(T)| \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for some absolute \(\delta>0\), and \(|P_i|\to\infty\) because \(d_i\to\infty\).

Below is a detailed proof following the high-degree \(S\)-unit strategy. I will identify \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\), so Euclidean distance is complex modulus.

The proof has one standard arithmetic input: an infinite totally real class-field tower in which one fixed rational prime \(p\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) splits completely. I state and justify this input first, then give the unit-distance construction.

1. Arithmetic tower with a fixed completely split prime

We need the following standard consequence of Golod–Shafarevich theory with prescribed splitting.

Lemma 1.1 — Totally real bounded-root-discriminant tower with a split prime

There exist a rational prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), a constant \(D_F>0\), and an infinite sequence of totally real number fields

\[ F_1,F_2,\dots \]

such that, writing \(d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\),

  1. \(d_i\to\infty\);
  2. \(\operatorname{rd}(F_i):=|\Delta_{F_i}|^{1/d_i}\le D_F\) for every \(i\);
  3. \(p\) splits completely in every \(F_i\).

One may take \(p=5\).

Justification of Lemma 1.1

This is the prescribed-splitting form of the Golod–Shafarevich class-field-tower theorem. For completeness, here is a standard construction.

Take an odd auxiliary prime \(\ell\), for example \(\ell=3\), and take \(p=5\). Choose many rational primes

\[ q_1,\dots,q_t \]

which split completely in \(\mathbb Q(\zeta_\ell,\sqrt[\ell]{p})\). By Chebotarev, there are infinitely many such primes. For each such \(q_j\), we have

\[ q_j\equiv 1\pmod \ell \]

and \(p\) is an \(\ell\)-th power modulo \(q_j\).

For each \(j\), choose a nontrivial Dirichlet character \(\chi_j\) of order \(\ell\) and conductor \(q_j\). Since \(p\) is an \(\ell\)-th power modulo \(q_j\),

\[ \chi_j(p)=1. \]

Also, because \(\ell\) is odd, \(\chi_j(-1)=1\), so the corresponding extension is totally real. Let

\[ \chi=\chi_1\cdots \chi_t. \]

The character \(\chi\) has order \(\ell\), conductor \(q_1\cdots q_t\), and satisfies \(\chi(p)=1\). Let \(F/\mathbb Q\) be the cyclic degree-\(\ell\) extension cut out by \(\chi\). Then:

  • \(F\) is totally real;
  • each \(q_j\) ramifies in \(F\);
  • \(p\) splits completely in \(F\).

Genus theory for cyclic prime-degree extensions gives

\[ \dim_{\mathbb F_\ell}\operatorname{Cl}(F)/\ell\operatorname{Cl}(F) \ge t-1. \]

Let \(T\) be the set of primes of \(F\) above \(p\). Since \(p\) splits completely in \(F\), we have \(|T|=\ell\).

Let \(G\) be the Galois group of the maximal everywhere-unramified pro-\(\ell\) extension of \(F\) in which every prime in \(T\) splits completely. Forcing the primes in \(T\) to split can reduce the generator rank by at most \(|T|\), so

\[ d(G)\ge t-1-|T|=t-1-\ell. \]

Shafarevich’s relation-rank bound gives

\[ r(G)\le d(G)+r_1(F)+|T|+1 = d(G)+2\ell+1, \]

because \(F\) is totally real of degree \(\ell\). Taking \(t\) sufficiently large makes

\[ r(G)<\frac{d(G)^2}{4}. \]

Therefore, by the Golod–Shafarevich inequality, \(G\) is infinite.

Let \(F_\infty/F\) be the corresponding infinite unramified pro-\(\ell\) extension with all primes over \(p\) split completely. Let \(F_i\) be finite subextensions with \([F_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty\). Since \(F_i/F\) is unramified at all finite primes,

\[ \operatorname{rd}(F_i)=\operatorname{rd}(F) \]

for every \(i\). Since \(\ell\) is odd and \(F\) is totally real, every finite subextension \(F_i\) is totally real. Finally, because \(p\) splits completely in \(F\) and every prime above \(p\) splits completely in \(F_i/F\), \(p\) splits completely in \(F_i\).

Thus Lemma 1.1 follows. \(\square\)

2. Passing to CM fields \(K_i=F_i(i)\)

Fix the sequence \(F_i\) from Lemma 1.1 and fix \(p=5\). For each \(i\), put

\[ d=d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q], \qquad K_i=F_i(i). \]

Let \(\tau\) denote complex conjugation on \(K_i/F_i\). Since \(F_i\) is totally real, \(K_i\) is a CM field of degree

\[ [K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d. \]

Because \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), \(p\) splits in \(\mathbb Q(i)\). Because \(p\) also splits completely in \(F_i\), it splits completely in \(K_i\). Thus

\[ p\mathcal O_{K_i} = \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak p_{i,j}\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}, \]

where

\[ \tau(\mathfrak p_{i,j})=\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j} \]

and every prime ideal appearing above has absolute norm \(p\).

The relative discriminant of \(K_i/F_i\) divides \(4\mathcal O_{F_i}\), so

\[ |\Delta_{K_i}| \le |\Delta_{F_i}|^2\,4^d. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i) = |\Delta_{K_i}|^{1/(2d)} \le 2\operatorname{rd}(F_i) \le 2D_F. \]

Fix once and for all a constant

\[ D\ge 2D_F \]

such that

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le D \]

for all \(i\).

3. A uniform class-number bound

We will use a very crude but uniform class-number estimate.

Lemma 3.1 — Exponential class-number bound

There is a constant \(H>1\), depending only on \(D\), such that

\[ h(K_i)\le H^d \]

for every \(i\), where \(d=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\).

Proof

Let \(K=K_i\). Then \([K:\mathbb Q]=2d\), \(r_2(K)=d\), and \(\operatorname{rd}(K)\le D\). The Minkowski bound says every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most

\[ M_K = \left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^d \frac{(2d)!}{(2d)^{2d}} \sqrt{|\Delta_K|}. \]

Since \((2d)!/(2d)^{2d}\le 1\) and

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_K|} = \operatorname{rd}(K)^d \le D^d, \]

we get

\[ M_K\le \left(\frac{4D}{\pi}\right)^d. \]

Put

\[ M_0=1+\frac{4D}{\pi}. \]

Then \(M_K\le M_0^d\).

Let \(a_K(n)\) be the number of integral ideals of \(K\) of norm \(n\). For \(s=2\),

\[ \sum_{n\le M_K} a_K(n) \le M_K^2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{a_K(n)}{n^2} = M_K^2\zeta_K(2). \]

For every number field of degree \(2d\),

\[ \zeta_K(2)\le \zeta(2)^{2d}. \]

Indeed, this follows by comparing Euler factors prime by prime.

Thus

\[ h(K) \le M_K^2\zeta_K(2) \le M_0^{2d}\zeta(2)^{2d}. \]

So it suffices to take

\[ H=\bigl(M_0\zeta(2)\bigr)^2. \]

\(\square\)

4. Many relative norm-one \(S\)-units

For a fixed \(i\), set

\[ S_i= \{\mathfrak p_{i,1},\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,1}, \dots, \mathfrak p_{i,d},\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,d}\}. \]

Let

\[ \mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times} \]

be the group of \(S_i\)-units of \(K_i\).

We will construct many elements \(u\in K_i^\times\) satisfying

\[ u\tau(u)=1. \]

For such \(u\), every complex embedding \(\sigma:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\) satisfies

\[ |\sigma(u)|=1. \]

Indeed,

\[ \sigma(\tau(u))=\overline{\sigma(u)}, \]

so

\[ 1=\sigma(u\tau(u))=\sigma(u)\overline{\sigma(u)} =|\sigma(u)|^2. \]

These elements will become unit-length translations.

4.1. Valuation lattice

Identify \(\mathbb Z^{S_i}\) with \(\mathbb Z^{2d}\) by writing a valuation vector as

\[ (a_1,b_1,\dots,a_d,b_d), \]

where \(a_j\) is the valuation at \(\mathfrak p_{i,j}\) and \(b_j\) is the valuation at \(\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}\).

Let

\[ V_i \subseteq \mathbb Z^{2d} \]

be the image of the valuation map

\[ \mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times} \longrightarrow \mathbb Z^{2d}. \]

Equivalently, \(V_i\) consists of all integer vectors \((a_j,b_j)\) such that

\[ \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak p_{i,j}^{a_j} \overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}^{b_j} \]

is principal.

Therefore the index of \(V_i\) in \(\mathbb Z^{2d}\) is at most the class number \(h(K_i)\). By Lemma 3.1,

\[ [\mathbb Z^{2d}:V_i]\le H^d. \]

Now define the difference map

\[ \partial:\mathbb Z^{2d}\to\mathbb Z^d \]

by

\[ \partial(a_1,b_1,\dots,a_d,b_d) = (a_1-b_1,\dots,a_d-b_d). \]

Put

\[ L_i=\partial(V_i)\subseteq\mathbb Z^d. \]

Since \(\partial\) is surjective,

\[ [\mathbb Z^d:L_i]\le [\mathbb Z^{2d}:V_i]\le H^d. \]

4.2. A subgroup-counting lemma

We will use the following elementary fact.

Lemma 4.1

Let \(L\subseteq\mathbb Z^d\) be a subgroup of index at most \(I\). Then for every integer \(m\ge1\),

\[ |L\cap[-2m,2m]^d| \ge \frac{(2m+1)^d}{I}. \]

Proof

Let

\[ Q=[-m,m]^d\cap\mathbb Z^d. \]

Then

\[ |Q|=(2m+1)^d. \]

Partition \(Q\) into cosets modulo \(L\). If \(a_c\) is the number of points of \(Q\) in a coset \(c\in \mathbb Z^d/L\), then

\[ \sum_c a_c=|Q|. \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \sum_c a_c^2 \ge \frac{|Q|^2}{[\mathbb Z^d:L]} \ge \frac{|Q|^2}{I}. \]

The quantity \(\sum_c a_c^2\) counts ordered pairs \((x,y)\in Q^2\) with \(x-y\in L\). Every such difference lies in

\[ L\cap[-2m,2m]^d. \]

For a fixed difference \(v\), there are at most \(|Q|\) ordered pairs \((x,y)\in Q^2\) with \(x-y=v\). Therefore

\[ |L\cap[-2m,2m]^d| \ge \frac{|Q|^2/I}{|Q|} = \frac{|Q|}{I} = \frac{(2m+1)^d}{I}. \]

\(\square\)

4.3. Choosing the valuation bound

Choose an integer \(m\ge1\) so large that

\[ C_U:=\frac{2m+1}{H} > \frac{16D}{\pi}. \]

Set

\[ B=2m. \]

For every \(i\), Lemma 4.1 applied to \(L_i\) gives

\[ |L_i\cap[-B,B]^d| = |L_i\cap[-2m,2m]^d| \ge \left(\frac{2m+1}{H}\right)^d = C_U^d. \]

For each vector

\[ n=(n_1,\dots,n_d)\in L_i\cap[-B,B]^d, \]

choose an \(S_i\)-unit \(y_n\in\mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times}\) whose valuation vector \(v(y_n)\in V_i\) satisfies

\[ \partial(v(y_n))=n. \]

Define

\[ u_n=\frac{y_n}{\tau(y_n)}. \]

Then

\[ u_n\tau(u_n)=1. \]

Moreover,

\[ v_{\mathfrak p_{i,j}}(u_n)=n_j, \qquad v_{\overline{\mathfrak p}_{i,j}}(u_n)=-n_j. \]

Thus \(u_n\) has valuation between \(-B\) and \(B\) at every prime in \(S_i\), and valuation \(0\) outside \(S_i\).

Distinct vectors \(n\) give distinct \(u_n\), because their valuations at the primes \(\mathfrak p_{i,j}\) are different. Therefore the set

\[ U_i:=\{u_n:n\in L_i\cap[-B,B]^d\} \]

satisfies

\[ |U_i|\ge C_U^d. \]

Every element \(u\in U_i\) satisfies

\[ u\tau(u)=1, \]

and hence has absolute value \(1\) in every complex embedding of \(K_i\).

5. A large finite set stable under those translations

Define the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak a_i = \prod_{\mathfrak q\in S_i}\mathfrak q^{-B}. \]

Because every prime in \(S_i\) has norm \(p\), and because \(|S_i|=2d\), we have

\[ N(\mathfrak a_i)=p^{-2Bd}. \]

Every element \(u\in U_i\) belongs to \(\mathfrak a_i\), because all of its valuations at primes in \(S_i\) are at least \(-B\), and its valuations outside \(S_i\) are \(0\).

Let

\[ \sigma_{i,1},\dots,\sigma_{i,d}:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C \]

be the \(d\) complex embeddings extending the \(d\) real embeddings of \(F_i\) and sending \(i\mapsto i\). Define the Minkowski embedding

\[ \Phi_i:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C^d \]

by

\[ \Phi_i(x)= (\sigma_{i,1}(x),\dots,\sigma_{i,d}(x)). \]

For \(R>1\), define the polydisc

\[ \mathcal B_d(R) = \{(z_1,\dots,z_d)\in\mathbb C^d: |z_j|\le R \text{ for all }j\}. \]

Now set

\[ A_i = \{x\in\mathfrak a_i:\Phi_i(x)\in\mathcal B_d(R)\}, \]

and

\[ A_i^\circ = \{x\in\mathfrak a_i:\Phi_i(x)\in\mathcal B_d(R-1)\}. \]

Thus \(A_i^\circ\subseteq A_i\).

6. Lattice-point estimates for \(A_i\)

We need an upper bound for \(|A_i|\) and a lower bound for \(|A_i^\circ|\), both exponential in \(d\) with fixed bases.

6.1. Lower bound for \(|A_i^\circ|\)

The lattice \(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)\subset\mathbb C^d\cong\mathbb R^{2d}\) has covolume

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)) = 2^{-d}\sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}\,N(\mathfrak a_i). \]

Since \(\operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le D\),

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}\le D^d. \]

Also,

\[ N(\mathfrak a_i)=p^{-2Bd}. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)) \le 2^{-d}D^d p^{-2Bd}. \]

The volume of \(\mathcal B_d(r)\) is

\[ \operatorname{vol}(\mathcal B_d(r))=(\pi r^2)^d. \]

We use the following elementary consequence of Blichfeldt’s lemma:

If \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n\) is a lattice and \(K\subset\mathbb R^n\) is a centrally symmetric convex body, then

\[ |\Lambda\cap K| \ge \frac{\operatorname{vol}(K)}{2^n\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Here \(n=2d\), so \(2^n=4^d\). Applying this with

\[ \Lambda=\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i), \qquad K=\mathcal B_d(R-1), \]

we get

\[ |A_i^\circ| \ge \frac{(\pi(R-1)^2)^d} {4^d\cdot 2^{-d}D^d p^{-2Bd}} = \left( \frac{\pi(R-1)^2p^{2B}}{2D} \right)^d. \]

Define

\[ C_I= \frac{\pi(R-1)^2p^{2B}}{2D}. \]

Then

\[ |A_i^\circ|\ge C_I^d. \]

6.2. Upper bound for \(|A_i|\)

We now prove a packing bound. Let \(x,y\in\mathfrak a_i\) be distinct, and put \(z=x-y\). Then \(z\in\mathfrak a_i\setminus\{0\}\).

Since \(z\in\mathfrak a_i\), the fractional ideal \((z)\mathfrak a_i^{-1}\) is an integral ideal. Therefore

\[ \frac{|N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)|}{N(\mathfrak a_i)} = N\bigl((z)\mathfrak a_i^{-1}\bigr) \]

is a positive integer. Hence

\[ |N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)| \ge N(\mathfrak a_i) = p^{-2Bd}. \]

But

\[ |N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)| = \prod_{j=1}^d |\sigma_{i,j}(z)|^2. \]

Thus if

\[ \max_j|\sigma_{i,j}(z)|<p^{-B}, \]

then

\[ |N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)| < p^{-2Bd}, \]

a contradiction. Therefore distinct points of \(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)\) are separated in sup-norm by at least \(p^{-B}\).

Consequently, the polydiscs of radius \(p^{-B}/2\) centered at the points of \(\Phi_i(A_i)\) are disjoint and all lie inside \(\mathcal B_d(R+p^{-B}/2)\). Hence

\[ |A_i| \cdot \left(\pi\left(\frac{p^{-B}}2\right)^2\right)^d \le \left(\pi\left(R+\frac{p^{-B}}2\right)^2\right)^d. \]

Therefore

\[ |A_i| \le (1+2Rp^B)^{2d}. \]

Define

\[ C_N=(1+2Rp^B)^2. \]

Then

\[ |A_i|\le C_N^d. \]

7. Choosing \(R\)

Recall that \(m\) was chosen so that

\[ C_U=\frac{2m+1}{H}>\frac{16D}{\pi}. \]

We now choose \(R>1\) sufficiently large so that

\[ \lambda:= \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N} >1. \]

This is possible because

\[ \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N} = \frac{ \left(\frac{\pi(R-1)^2p^{2B}}{2D}\right)C_U } {(1+2Rp^B)^2}, \]

and as \(R\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N} \longrightarrow \frac{\pi C_U}{8D}. \]

Since \(C_U>16D/\pi\), the limiting value is \(>2\). Thus we can choose \(R\) so large that

\[ \lambda>1. \]

We also enlarge \(R\), if necessary, so that

\[ C_I>1. \]

All constants

\[ p,D,H,m,B,R,C_U,C_I,C_N,\lambda \]

are now fixed once and for all, independently of \(i\).

8. Producing unit distances in the plane

Fix \(i\). Let \(d=d_i\). We have finite sets

\[ A_i^\circ\subseteq A_i\subset K_i \]

and a set \(U_i\subset K_i^\times\) satisfying:

  1. \(|A_i^\circ|\ge C_I^d\);
  2. \(|A_i|\le C_N^d\);
  3. \(|U_i|\ge C_U^d\);
  4. every \(u\in U_i\) satisfies \(u\tau(u)=1\);
  5. every \(u\in U_i\) belongs to \(\mathfrak a_i\).

For \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and \(u\in U_i\), we claim

\[ x+u\in A_i. \]

Indeed, \(x,u\in\mathfrak a_i\), so \(x+u\in\mathfrak a_i\). Also, for every \(j\),

\[ |\sigma_{i,j}(x+u)| \le |\sigma_{i,j}(x)|+|\sigma_{i,j}(u)|. \]

Since \(x\in A_i^\circ\),

\[ |\sigma_{i,j}(x)|\le R-1. \]

Since \(u\tau(u)=1\),

\[ |\sigma_{i,j}(u)|=1. \]

Hence

\[ |\sigma_{i,j}(x+u)|\le R. \]

Thus \(x+u\in A_i\).

Now project to one complex embedding, say \(\sigma_{i,1}\). Define

\[ P_i=\sigma_{i,1}(A_i)\subset\mathbb C\simeq\mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\sigma_{i,1}\) is an embedding, it is injective, so

\[ |P_i|=|A_i|. \]

For every \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and \(u\in U_i\), the two points

\[ \sigma_{i,1}(x), \qquad \sigma_{i,1}(x+u) \]

belong to \(P_i\), and their distance is

\[ |\sigma_{i,1}(x+u)-\sigma_{i,1}(x)| = |\sigma_{i,1}(u)| = 1. \]

Distinct pairs \((x,u)\) give distinct ordered unit-distance pairs, because \(\sigma_{i,1}\) is injective and the difference determines \(u\). Therefore the number of ordered unit-distance pairs in \(P_i\) is at least

\[ |A_i^\circ|\,|U_i|. \]

Since \(u(P_i)\) counts unoriented pairs, we have

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 |A_i^\circ|\,|U_i|. \]

Using the bounds above,

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 C_I^d C_U^d = \frac12 (C_I C_U)^d. \]

Since

\[ C_I C_U=\lambda C_N, \]

we get

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12(\lambda C_N)^d. \]

On the other hand,

\[ |P_i| = |A_i| \le C_N^d. \]

9. Extracting the exponent \(\delta>0\)

Define

\[ \delta = \frac12\cdot\frac{\log\lambda}{\log C_N}. \]

Since \(\lambda>1\), we have

\[ \delta>0. \]

Choose \(i\) so large that

\[ \lambda^{d_i/2}\ge 2. \]

This holds for all sufficiently large \(i\), since \(d_i\to\infty\). Discarding finitely many initial terms and reindexing, we may assume it holds for every \(i\).

Then

\[ \frac12\lambda^{d_i} \ge \lambda^{d_i/2} = C_N^{\delta d_i}. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12(\lambda C_N)^{d_i} = C_N^{d_i}\cdot \frac12\lambda^{d_i} \ge C_N^{(1+\delta)d_i}. \]

Since

\[ |P_i|\le C_N^{d_i}, \]

we obtain

\[ u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

Finally, since \(C_I>1\),

\[ |P_i| = |A_i| \ge |A_i^\circ| \ge C_I^{d_i} \to\infty. \]

Thus the sequence \(\{P_i\}\) satisfies

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for a fixed absolute constant \(\delta>0\).

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

Summary of the mechanism

The proof uses three ingredients.

  1. Many split primes in high degree. A fixed rational prime \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) splits into \(2d\) prime ideals in the CM field \(K_i=F_i(i)\).

  2. Many relative norm-one \(S\)-units. The \(S\)-unit group has enough valuation freedom to produce at least \(C_U^d\) elements \(u\) satisfying

    \[ u\tau(u)=1. \]

    Hence every such \(u\) has complex absolute value \(1\) in every embedding.

  3. A finite adelic box stable under these translations. The fractional ideal

    \[ \mathfrak a_i=\prod_{\mathfrak q\mid p}\mathfrak q^{-B} \]

    contains all these unit translations. The set of elements of \(\mathfrak a_i\) bounded by \(R\) in every complex embedding has size \(\le C_N^d\), while its interior has size \(\ge C_I^d\). Every interior point translated by any \(u\in U_i\) remains in the outer set.

The inequality

\[ \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N}>1 \]

then gives a genuine fixed-power gain over linear many unit distances.

\[ r(G)>\frac{d(G)^2}{4}. \]

But since \(d(G)\ge g_0\), our choice of \(g_0\) gives

\[ d(G)+7<\frac{d(G)^2}{4}, \]

contradiction. Hence \(G\) is infinite.

Let \(F_i\) be finite subextensions of this infinite pro-\(3\) extension with

\[ [F_i:F]\to\infty. \]

Because the tower is unramified at all finite primes,

\[ \operatorname{rd}(F_i)=\operatorname{rd}(F) \]

for every \(i\). Since the extension is pro-\(3\), no real place can become complex in a finite Galois subextension; thus every \(F_i\) is totally real. Finally, every prime above \(5\) splits completely throughout the tower, so \(5\) splits completely in every \(F_i\).

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

2. Passing to CM fields

For each \(i\), put

\[ n=n_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q] \]

and define

\[ K_i=F_i(i). \]

Let \(\tau\) denote complex conjugation on \(K_i/F_i\).

Since \(F_i\) is totally real, \(K_i\) is a CM field of degree

\[ [K_i:\mathbb Q]=2n. \]

Since \(5\equiv1\pmod4\), the prime \(5\) splits in \(\mathbb Q(i)\). Since \(5\) also splits completely in \(F_i\), it splits completely in \(K_i\). Thus we may write

\[ 5\mathcal O_{K_i} = \prod_{j=1}^{n} \mathfrak p_j\overline{\mathfrak p}_j, \]

where

\[ \tau(\mathfrak p_j)=\overline{\mathfrak p}_j \]

and

\[ N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(\mathfrak p_j) = N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(\overline{\mathfrak p}_j) = 5. \]

Also, the relative discriminant of \(K_i/F_i\) divides \(4\mathcal O_{F_i}\), because \(K_i=F_i(i)\). Hence

\[ |\Delta_{K_i}| \le |\Delta_{F_i}|^2\,4^n. \]

Since the root discriminants of the \(F_i\) are bounded, there is a constant \(\mathcal D>0\) such that

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i) = |\Delta_{K_i}|^{1/(2n)} \le \mathcal D \]

for all \(i\).

3. A uniform class-number bound

We need a crude exponential bound for \(h(K_i)\).

Lemma 3.1

There exists a constant \(H>1\), independent of \(i\), such that

\[ h(K_i)\le H^n. \]

Proof

Let \(K=K_i\). Then \([K:\mathbb Q]=2n\), \(r_2(K)=n\), and \(\operatorname{rd}(K)\le\mathcal D\).

Minkowski’s bound says every ideal class of \(K\) contains an integral ideal of norm at most

\[ M_K= \left(\frac4\pi\right)^n \frac{(2n)!}{(2n)^{2n}} \sqrt{|\Delta_K|}. \]

Since

\[ \frac{(2n)!}{(2n)^{2n}}\le1 \]

and

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_K|} = \operatorname{rd}(K)^n \le \mathcal D^n, \]

we get

\[ M_K\le \left(\frac{4\mathcal D}{\pi}\right)^n. \]

Set

\[ M_0=1+\frac{4\mathcal D}{\pi}. \]

Then \(M_K\le M_0^n\).

Let \(a_K(m)\) be the number of integral ideals of \(K\) of norm \(m\). Since every ideal class contains an ideal of norm at most \(M_K\),

\[ h(K) \le \sum_{m\le M_K} a_K(m). \]

For \(m\le M_K\le M_0^n\), we have

\[ 1\le \frac{M_0^{2n}}{m^2}. \]

Therefore

\[ h(K) \le M_0^{2n}\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{a_K(m)}{m^2} = M_0^{2n}\zeta_K(2). \]

Finally,

\[ \zeta_K(2)\le \zeta(2)^{2n}, \]

because \([K:\mathbb Q]=2n\). Hence

\[ h(K)\le \left(M_0^2\zeta(2)^2\right)^n. \]

Take

\[ H=M_0^2\zeta(2)^2. \]

\(\square\)

4. Many norm-one \(S\)-units

Fix \(i\), and write

\[ S_i= \{\mathfrak p_1,\overline{\mathfrak p}_1,\dots, \mathfrak p_n,\overline{\mathfrak p}_n\}. \]

Let

\[ \mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times} \]

be the group of \(S_i\)-units.

For \(y\in\mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times}\), write its valuation vector as

\[ v(y)= (a_1,b_1,\dots,a_n,b_n)\in\mathbb Z^{2n}, \]

where

\[ a_j=v_{\mathfrak p_j}(y), \qquad b_j=v_{\overline{\mathfrak p}_j}(y). \]

Let

\[ V_i\subseteq\mathbb Z^{2n} \]

be the image of the valuation map.

The quotient \(\mathbb Z^{2n}/V_i\) injects into the ideal class group: a vector \((a_j,b_j)\) belongs to \(V_i\) exactly when

\[ \prod_{j=1}^n \mathfrak p_j^{a_j} \overline{\mathfrak p}_j^{b_j} \]

is principal. Therefore

\[ [\mathbb Z^{2n}:V_i]\le h(K_i)\le H^n. \]

Define

\[ \partial:\mathbb Z^{2n}\to\mathbb Z^n \]

by

\[ \partial(a_1,b_1,\dots,a_n,b_n) = (a_1-b_1,\dots,a_n-b_n). \]

Let

\[ L_i=\partial(V_i)\subseteq\mathbb Z^n. \]

Since \(\partial\) is surjective,

\[ [\mathbb Z^n:L_i]\le [\mathbb Z^{2n}:V_i]\le H^n. \]

We now use a simple counting lemma.

Lemma 4.1

Let \(L\subseteq\mathbb Z^n\) be a subgroup of index at most \(I\). Then for every integer \(m\ge1\),

\[ |L\cap[-2m,2m]^n| \ge \frac{(2m+1)^n}{I}. \]

Proof

Let

\[ Q=[-m,m]^n\cap\mathbb Z^n. \]

Then \(|Q|=(2m+1)^n\). Partition \(Q\) into cosets modulo \(L\). If \(a_c\) is the number of points of \(Q\) in the coset \(c\), then

\[ \sum_c a_c=|Q|. \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[ \sum_c a_c^2 \ge \frac{|Q|^2}{[\mathbb Z^n:L]} \ge \frac{|Q|^2}{I}. \]

The quantity \(\sum_c a_c^2\) counts ordered pairs \((x,y)\in Q^2\) with \(x-y\in L\). Every such difference lies in

\[ L\cap[-2m,2m]^n. \]

For each fixed difference \(z\), there are at most \(|Q|\) pairs \((x,y)\in Q^2\) with \(x-y=z\). Therefore

\[ |L\cap[-2m,2m]^n| \ge \frac{|Q|^2/I}{|Q|} = \frac{(2m+1)^n}{I}. \]

\(\square\)

Choose an integer \(m\) so large that

\[ C_U:=\frac{2m+1}{H} > \frac{16\mathcal D}{\pi}. \]

Set

\[ B=2m. \]

Applying Lemma 4.1 to \(L_i\), we get

\[ |L_i\cap[-B,B]^n| \ge C_U^n. \]

For every vector

\[ \mathbf a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)\in L_i\cap[-B,B]^n, \]

choose \(y_{\mathbf a}\in\mathcal O_{K_i,S_i}^{\times}\) whose valuation vector lies in \(V_i\) and whose image under \(\partial\) is \(\mathbf a\). Define

\[ u_{\mathbf a} = \frac{y_{\mathbf a}}{\tau(y_{\mathbf a})}. \]

Then

\[ u_{\mathbf a}\tau(u_{\mathbf a})=1. \]

Moreover,

\[ v_{\mathfrak p_j}(u_{\mathbf a})=a_j, \qquad v_{\overline{\mathfrak p}_j}(u_{\mathbf a})=-a_j. \]

Thus every valuation of \(u_{\mathbf a}\) at primes in \(S_i\) lies between \(-B\) and \(B\), and \(u_{\mathbf a}\) has valuation \(0\) outside \(S_i\).

Distinct \(\mathbf a\) give distinct \(u_{\mathbf a}\), because their valuations at the primes \(\mathfrak p_j\) are distinct. Hence the set

\[ U_i= \{u_{\mathbf a}:\mathbf a\in L_i\cap[-B,B]^n\} \]

satisfies

\[ |U_i|\ge C_U^n. \]

Finally, if \(\sigma:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\) is any complex embedding extending a real embedding of \(F_i\), then

\[ \sigma(\tau(u))=\overline{\sigma(u)}. \]

Thus

\[ u\tau(u)=1 \implies |\sigma(u)|^2=1, \]

so every \(u\in U_i\) has complex absolute value \(1\) in every embedding.

5. The finite algebraic set of points

Define the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak a_i = \prod_{\mathfrak q\in S_i}\mathfrak q^{-B}. \]

Since every \(\mathfrak q\in S_i\) has norm \(5\), and \(|S_i|=2n\),

\[ N(\mathfrak a_i)=5^{-2Bn}. \]

Every \(u\in U_i\) belongs to \(\mathfrak a_i\), because all its valuations at primes in \(S_i\) are at least \(-B\), and all other valuations are nonnegative.

Let

\[ \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_n:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C \]

be the \(n\) embeddings extending the \(n\) real embeddings of \(F_i\) and sending \(i\mapsto i\). Define

\[ \Phi_i:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C^n \]

by

\[ \Phi_i(x)=(\sigma_1(x),\dots,\sigma_n(x)). \]

For \(R>1\), set

\[ \mathcal B_n(R) = \{(z_1,\dots,z_n)\in\mathbb C^n: |z_j|\le R\ \forall j\}. \]

Define

\[ A_i= \{x\in\mathfrak a_i:\Phi_i(x)\in\mathcal B_n(R)\} \]

and

\[ A_i^\circ= \{x\in\mathfrak a_i:\Phi_i(x)\in\mathcal B_n(R-1)\}. \]

Then \(A_i^\circ\subseteq A_i\).

If \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and \(u\in U_i\), then \(x,u\in\mathfrak a_i\), so

\[ x+u\in\mathfrak a_i. \]

Also, for every \(j\),

\[ |\sigma_j(x+u)| \le |\sigma_j(x)|+|\sigma_j(u)| \le (R-1)+1 = R. \]

Hence

\[ x+u\in A_i. \]

Thus every \(x\in A_i^\circ\) can be translated by every \(u\in U_i\) and remains inside \(A_i\).

6. Lattice-point estimates

We need upper and lower bounds for \(|A_i|\) and \(|A_i^\circ|\).

The lattice \(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)\subset\mathbb C^n\cong\mathbb R^{2n}\) has covolume

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)) = 2^{-n}\sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}\,N(\mathfrak a_i). \]

Since \(\operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le\mathcal D\),

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_{K_i}|}\le \mathcal D^n. \]

Thus

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)) \le 2^{-n}\mathcal D^n5^{-2Bn}. \]

Lower bound for \(|A_i^\circ|\)

We use the following standard Blichfeldt consequence.

If \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^N\) is a lattice and \(K\subset\mathbb R^N\) is centrally symmetric and convex, then

\[ |\Lambda\cap K| \ge \frac{\operatorname{vol}(K)}{2^N\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Indeed, Blichfeldt gives a translate of \(\frac12K\) containing at least

\[ \frac{\operatorname{vol}(K)}{2^N\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)} \]

lattice points. Subtracting one of them from all the others gives distinct lattice points in \(K\).

Here \(N=2n\), and

\[ \operatorname{vol}(\mathcal B_n(r))=(\pi r^2)^n. \]

Applying the bound to

\[ \Lambda=\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i), \qquad K=\mathcal B_n(R-1), \]

we get

\[ |A_i^\circ| \ge \frac{(\pi(R-1)^2)^n} {4^n\cdot 2^{-n}\mathcal D^n5^{-2Bn}}. \]

Therefore

\[ |A_i^\circ| \ge \left( \frac{\pi(R-1)^2 5^{2B}}{2\mathcal D} \right)^n. \]

Set

\[ C_I= \frac{\pi(R-1)^2 5^{2B}}{2\mathcal D}. \]

Then

\[ |A_i^\circ|\ge C_I^n. \]

Upper bound for \(|A_i|\)

Let \(x,y\in\mathfrak a_i\) be distinct, and set

\[ z=x-y\neq0. \]

Then \(z\in\mathfrak a_i\). Since \(z\in\mathfrak a_i\), the fractional ideal

\[ (z)\mathfrak a_i^{-1} \]

is integral. Hence

\[ \frac{|N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)|}{N(\mathfrak a_i)} = N\bigl((z)\mathfrak a_i^{-1}\bigr) \]

is a positive integer. Therefore

\[ |N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)| \ge N(\mathfrak a_i) = 5^{-2Bn}. \]

But

\[ |N_{K_i/\mathbb Q}(z)| = \prod_{j=1}^{n}|\sigma_j(z)|^2. \]

Thus it is impossible that

\[ |\sigma_j(z)|<5^{-B} \]

for every \(j\). Therefore distinct points of \(\Phi_i(\mathfrak a_i)\) are separated in sup norm by at least \(5^{-B}\).

Consequently, the open polydiscs of radius \(5^{-B}/2\) around the points of \(\Phi_i(A_i)\) are pairwise disjoint. They all lie inside

\[ \mathcal B_n\left(R+\frac{5^{-B}}2\right). \]

Comparing volumes gives

\[ |A_i| \left(\pi\left(\frac{5^{-B}}2\right)^2\right)^n \le \left(\pi\left(R+\frac{5^{-B}}2\right)^2\right)^n. \]

Hence

\[ |A_i| \le (1+2R5^B)^{2n}. \]

Set

\[ C_N=(1+2R5^B)^2. \]

Then

\[ |A_i|\le C_N^n. \]

7. Choosing \(R\)

Recall that \(m\) was chosen so that

\[ C_U>\frac{16\mathcal D}{\pi}. \]

We now choose \(R>1\) sufficiently large so that

\[ \lambda:= \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N}>1. \]

Indeed,

\[ \frac{C_I C_U}{C_N} = \frac{ \left(\frac{\pi(R-1)^2 5^{2B}}{2\mathcal D}\right)C_U } {(1+2R5^B)^2}. \]

As \(R\to\infty\), this tends to

\[ \frac{\pi C_U}{8\mathcal D}. \]

By our choice of \(C_U\),

\[ \frac{\pi C_U}{8\mathcal D}>2. \]

So for all sufficiently large \(R\),

\[ \lambda>1. \]

We also enlarge \(R\), if necessary, so that

\[ C_I>1. \]

All constants

\[ B,\ R,\ C_U,\ C_I,\ C_N,\ \lambda \]

are now fixed independently of \(i\).

8. Projecting to the plane

Fix one embedding, say \(\sigma_1\), and define

\[ P_i=\sigma_1(A_i)\subset\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2. \]

Since \(\sigma_1\) is injective,

\[ |P_i|=|A_i|. \]

For every \(x\in A_i^\circ\) and every \(u\in U_i\), we know \(x+u\in A_i\). Therefore

\[ \sigma_1(x),\ \sigma_1(x+u)\in P_i. \]

Their distance is

\[ |\sigma_1(x+u)-\sigma_1(x)| = |\sigma_1(u)| = 1. \]

Moreover, the map

\[ (x,u)\mapsto \bigl(\sigma_1(x),\sigma_1(x+u)\bigr) \]

is injective. Indeed, if two pairs \((x,u)\) and \((x',u')\) give the same ordered pair, then injectivity of \(\sigma_1\) gives \(x=x'\) and \(x+u=x'+u'\), hence \(u=u'\).

Thus the number of ordered unit-distance pairs in \(P_i\) is at least

\[ |A_i^\circ|\,|U_i|. \]

Since \(u(P_i)\) counts unoriented unit-distance pairs,

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12|A_i^\circ|\,|U_i|. \]

Using the estimates above,

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 C_I^n C_U^n = \frac12(C_I C_U)^n. \]

By definition of \(\lambda\),

\[ C_I C_U=\lambda C_N. \]

Hence

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12(\lambda C_N)^n. \]

Also,

\[ |P_i|=|A_i|\le C_N^n. \]

9. Extracting a fixed positive exponent

Define

\[ \delta= \frac12\cdot\frac{\log\lambda}{\log C_N}. \]

Since \(\lambda>1\), we have

\[ \delta>0. \]

For all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ \lambda^{n_i/2}\ge2. \]

Discard finitely many initial fields and relabel the remaining sequence. Then for every remaining \(i\),

\[ \frac12\lambda^{n_i}\ge \lambda^{n_i/2}. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12(\lambda C_N)^{n_i} = C_N^{n_i}\cdot \frac12\lambda^{n_i} \ge C_N^{n_i}\lambda^{n_i/2}. \]

By the definition of \(\delta\),

\[ \lambda^{n_i/2} = C_N^{\delta n_i}. \]

So

\[ u(P_i)\ge C_N^{(1+\delta)n_i}. \]

Since

\[ |P_i|\le C_N^{n_i}, \]

we conclude

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

Finally, because \(C_I>1\),

\[ |P_i| = |A_i| \ge |A_i^\circ| \ge C_I^{n_i} \to\infty. \]

Thus the finite point sets \(P_i\subset\mathbb R^2\) satisfy

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for a fixed absolute constant \(\delta>0\).

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)
