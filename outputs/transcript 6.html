Theorem. There exist an absolute constant $\delta > 0$ and an infinite sequence $\{P_i\}$ of finite point sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $|P_i| \to \infty$ and $u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}$ for all $i$, where $u(P_i) = \frac{1}{2} | \{ (p,q) \in P_i \times P_i : \|p-q\|_2 = 1 \} |$ is the number of unit-distance pairs.

Remark. This result disproves the Erdős Unit Distance Conjecture.

Assistant

Below are the three approaches I would pursue first. A common theme is that one should not try to put the points in a separated Euclidean lattice; rather, one should exploit a high-rank additive subgroup of \(\mathbb R^2\) that is very “compressed” in the physical plane. The most promising route, in my view, is an arithmetic one using norm-one \(S\)-units in high-degree number fields.

A guiding reduction: many unit translations in a low-complexity additive set

Let \(\Gamma \subset \mathbb R^2\) be an additive subgroup, and suppose we can find many vectors

\[ U \subseteq \Gamma \cap S^1 \]

of Euclidean norm \(1\). If \(A \subset \Gamma\) is finite and has small boundary with respect to every \(u \in U\), meaning

\[ |A \cap (A-u)| \ge \eta |A| \]

for all \(u \in U\), then the number of directed unit-distance pairs in \(A\) is at least

\[ \sum_{u\in U} |A\cap(A-u)| \ge \eta |U|\, |A|. \]

After dividing by \(2\) to account for orientations, one gets

\[ u(A) \gtrsim |U|\, |A|. \]

Thus, if one can arrange

\[ |U| \ge |A|^\delta \]

for some absolute \(\delta>0\), then

\[ u(A) \gtrsim |A|^{1+\delta}. \]

So the problem becomes:

Construct finite additive sets \(A\subset \mathbb R^2\) that are Følner-like for exponentially many unit translations.

The three approaches below are different ways to produce such \(A\) and \(U\).

Approach 1: High-degree number fields and norm-one \(S\)-units

Summary

Use a tower of totally real number fields \(K_i\) of degrees \(k_i\to\infty\), with bounded root discriminant and with many degree-one primes above a fixed rational prime \(p\equiv 1 \pmod 4\). In \(L_i=K_i(i)\), these primes split into conjugate pairs. Quotients of conjugate ideals produce many elements \(z\in L_i^\times\) satisfying

\[ z\overline z = 1. \]

Writing

\[ z = x+iy,\qquad x,y\in K_i, \]

we get

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

After choosing one real embedding \(\sigma:K_i\hookrightarrow \mathbb R\), each such \(z\) gives a genuine Euclidean unit vector

\[ v_z = \bigl(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)\bigr)\in \mathbb R^2. \]

The additive point set comes from a large finite chunk of the fractional ideal \(D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}\), viewed through the same embedding \(\sigma\). Because the unit vectors have all conjugates bounded by \(1\), a translated Minkowski box is stable under all of these translations, giving many unit distances.

This is the approach I would rank as the most promising.

1.1. Arithmetic input: a tower with many split small primes

We want a sequence of totally real fields \(K_i\) such that:

  1. \(k_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty\);

  2. the root discriminants are uniformly bounded:

    \[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)=|\operatorname{disc}(K_i)|^{1/k_i}\le C; \]
  3. for some fixed rational prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the field \(K_i\) has at least \(\rho k_i\) degree-one primes above \(p\), for some fixed \(\rho>0\).

The strongest version is that \(p\) splits completely in each \(K_i\), in which case \(\rho=1\). Such towers are usually constructed using Golod–Shafarevich/class-field-tower methods, with prescribed splitting conditions.

Let

\[ L_i=K_i(i). \]

Since \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the polynomial \(X^2+1\) splits modulo \(p\). Therefore every degree-one prime \(\mathfrak p\subset \mathcal O_{K_i}\) above \(p\) splits in \(L_i\):

\[ \mathfrak p\mathcal O_{L_i} = \mathfrak P\,\overline{\mathfrak P}. \]

Choose \(s_i\ge \rho k_i\) such primes, giving conjugate pairs

\[ \mathfrak P_1,\overline{\mathfrak P}_1,\dots, \mathfrak P_{s_i},\overline{\mathfrak P}_{s_i}. \]

1.2. Producing exponentially many norm-one elements

Fix an integer \(E\ge 1\), independent of \(i\). For each vector

\[ a=(a_1,\dots,a_{s_i})\in \{0,1,\dots,E\}^{s_i}, \]

consider the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak A_a = \prod_{j=1}^{s_i} \mathfrak P_j^{a_j}\overline{\mathfrak P}_j^{E-a_j} \subset L_i. \]

There are

\[ (E+1)^{s_i} \]

such ideals.

Their ideal classes lie in \(\operatorname{Cl}(L_i)\). Since the root discriminants of \(L_i\) are also uniformly bounded, standard class-number estimates give

\[ h(L_i)=|\operatorname{Cl}(L_i)|\le H^{k_i} \]

for some constant \(H\).

By pigeonhole, some ideal class contains at least

\[ \frac{(E+1)^{s_i}}{h(L_i)} \ge \left(\frac{(E+1)^\rho}{H}\right)^{k_i} \]

of the ideals \(\mathfrak A_a\).

Choosing \(E\) large enough, we can make

\[ C_U:=\frac{(E+1)^\rho}{H}>1. \]

Fix one representative \(a^{(0)}\) in that large class. For every other representative \(a\) in the same class, the quotient

\[ \mathfrak A_a\mathfrak A_{a^{(0)}}^{-1} \]

is principal, say

\[ \mathfrak A_a\mathfrak A_{a^{(0)}}^{-1}=(\alpha_a). \]

Now define

\[ z_a=\frac{\alpha_a}{\overline{\alpha_a}}. \]

Then

\[ z_a\overline{z_a}=1. \]

Moreover, the valuations of \(z_a\) are supported only over primes above \(p\), and the exponents are bounded by \(2E\). Thus, for a fixed integer

\[ D=2p^{2E}, \]

we have

\[ x_a:=\frac{z_a+\overline{z_a}}{2}\in D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}, \]

and

\[ y_a:=\frac{z_a-\overline{z_a}}{2i}\in D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}. \]

Also,

\[ x_a^2+y_a^2=1. \]

Therefore, under any real embedding \(\tau:K_i\hookrightarrow \mathbb R\),

\[ \tau(x_a)^2+\tau(y_a)^2=1, \]

so

\[ |\tau(x_a)|\le 1,\qquad |\tau(y_a)|\le 1. \]

Thus we get at least

\[ M_i\ge C_U^{k_i} \]

distinct unit vectors

\[ v_a=\bigl(\sigma(x_a),\sigma(y_a)\bigr)\in \mathbb R^2. \]

This is the key exponential supply of unit directions.

1.3. Building the finite point set

Let

\[ \Lambda_i=D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}. \]

Let \(\Phi_i:K_i\to \mathbb R^{k_i}\) be the Minkowski embedding, i.e.

\[ \Phi_i(x)=(\tau_1(x),\dots,\tau_{k_i}(x)). \]

For \(R>1\) and a translate \(t\in\mathbb R^{k_i}\), define

\[ B_i(R,t) = \{x\in \Lambda_i:\Phi_i(x)\in t+[-R,R]^{k_i}\}. \]

We choose \(t=t_i\) so that the inner box \(B_i(R-1,t_i)\) contains many lattice points. By averaging over a fundamental domain of \(\Phi_i(\Lambda_i)\), one obtains

\[ |B_i(R-1,t_i)|\ge C_-^{k_i} \]

for some \(C_->1\), provided \(R\) is chosen sufficiently large. Uniform boundedness of the root discriminants gives an upper bound

\[ |B_i(R,t_i)|\le C_+^{k_i} \]

for a constant \(C_+<\infty\).

Now define the planar point set

\[ P_i = \left\{ \bigl(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)\bigr): x,y\in B_i(R,t_i) \right\} \subset \mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\sigma\) is injective on \(K_i\), the size of \(P_i\) is exactly

\[ |P_i|=|B_i(R,t_i)|^2. \]

Thus

\[ C_-^{2k_i} \le |P_i| \le C_+^{2k_i}. \]

In particular, \(|P_i|\to\infty\).

1.4. Counting unit distances

For each norm-one element \(z_a=x_a+iy_a\), we know that

\[ x_a,y_a\in \Lambda_i \]

and

\[ |\tau(x_a)|,|\tau(y_a)|\le 1 \]

for every real embedding \(\tau\).

Therefore, if \(x,y\in B_i(R-1,t_i)\), then

\[ x+x_a,\; y+y_a\in B_i(R,t_i). \]

Thus the two points

\[ \bigl(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)\bigr) \]

and

\[ \bigl(\sigma(x+x_a),\sigma(y+y_a)\bigr) \]

both lie in \(P_i\), and their Euclidean distance is

\[ \sqrt{\sigma(x_a)^2+\sigma(y_a)^2}=1. \]

So each unit vector \(v_a\) contributes at least

\[ |B_i(R-1,t_i)|^2 \]

directed unit-distance pairs.

Since there are at least \(M_i\ge C_U^{k_i}\) such vectors, we get

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 C_U^{k_i}|B_i(R-1,t_i)|^2 \ge \frac12 (C_U C_-^2)^{k_i}. \]

Meanwhile,

\[ |P_i|\le C_+^{2k_i}. \]

So if

\[ C_U C_-^2 > C_+^2, \]

then for some \(\delta>0\),

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

One can arrange this by choosing \(R\) large enough to make \(C_-/C_+\) close to \(1\), and choosing \(E\) large enough to make \(C_U>1\). The resulting \(\delta\) may be very small, but it is absolute.

1.5. Why this approach is so promising

This route has a clean division of labor:

  1. Class-field-tower input: produce \(K_i\) with bounded root discriminant and many degree-one primes above a fixed \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\).
  2. Ideal-class pigeonhole: produce exponentially many norm-one \(S\)-units \(z\).
  3. Minkowski box/Følner set: turn those unit directions into many unit distances.

Everything after the tower input is relatively robust. The construction also naturally respects the known upper bound \(u(P)=O(|P|^{4/3})\), because the exponent gain \(\delta\) is small and tunable.

This is the approach I would try to turn into a full proof first.

Approach 2: Parametrized conic over high-degree fields / local-to-global construction

Summary

The unit circle

\[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

is a rational curve. One can parametrize its \(K\)-points by

\[ x(t)=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \qquad y(t)=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}. \]

Thus, for a high-degree field \(K\), if one can find many parameters \(t\in K\) for which \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) lie in a controlled fractional ideal and have bounded conjugates, then the same Minkowski-box construction gives many unit distances.

This is morally close to Approach 1, but the attack is more “coordinate-based” and may lead to a more explicit construction.

2.1. Desired input

Let \(K_i\) be totally real of degree \(k_i\), and fix a fractional ideal \(\Lambda_i\subset K_i\). We want many \(t\in K_i\) such that

\[ x(t),y(t)\in \Lambda_i \]

and for every embedding \(\tau:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\),

\[ |\tau(x(t))|,|\tau(y(t))|\le 1. \]

The second condition is automatic if \(x(t)^2+y(t)^2=1\) inside \(K_i\), because each real embedding preserves the equation.

Thus the hard part is denominator control:

\[ \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2} \in D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i} \]

for a fixed \(D\) independent of \(i\).

If we can produce \(M_i\ge C^{k_i}\) such parameters, then the same argument as in Approach 1 gives

\[ u(P_i)\gtrsim M_i|P_i|, \]

with \(|P_i|\le C_0^{k_i}\), yielding a polynomial gain.

2.2. Using split primes and CRT

Suppose a rational prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\) has many degree-one primes in \(K_i\). Then

\[ \mathcal O_{K_i}/p\mathcal O_{K_i} \]

has many factors isomorphic to \(\mathbb F_p\). Locally modulo \(p\), the equation

\[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

has about \(p\) solutions in each coordinate factor. Therefore, modulo \(p\), one expects roughly

\[ p^{\rho k_i} \]

independent local choices.

The goal would be to lift many of these local choices to exact global solutions with denominator \(p^E\), i.e. solutions

\[ x,y\in p^{-E}\mathcal O_{K_i} \]

satisfying

\[ x^2+y^2=1. \]

Equivalently, in \(L_i=K_i(i)\), one wants many elements

\[ z=x+iy \]

satisfying

\[ z\overline z=1 \]

with controlled \(p\)-adic denominators.

This is precisely a norm-one torus problem. Approach 1 solves it via ideal classes and \(S\)-units. But this more local/CRT viewpoint may allow a more elementary or more explicit construction.

2.3. Function-field toy model

There is a very suggestive function-field analogue.

Work over \(\mathbb R(t)\), and choose non-real complex numbers

\[ z_1,\dots,z_s\in \mathbb C\setminus \mathbb R. \]

For each subset \(A\subseteq \{1,\dots,s\}\), define

\[ w_A(t) = \prod_{j\in A} \frac{t-z_j}{t-\overline{z_j}}. \]

For real \(t\), each factor has modulus \(1\), so

\[ |w_A(t)|=1. \]

Now set

\[ x_A(t)=\frac{w_A(t)+w_A(t)^{-1}}2, \qquad y_A(t)=\frac{w_A(t)-w_A(t)^{-1}}{2i}. \]

Then

\[ x_A(t)^2+y_A(t)^2=1. \]

There are \(2^s\) such unit directions, while their poles lie in a divisor of degree \(O(s)\). This is exactly the desired exponential-in-dimension supply of unit directions.

The obstruction is integrality/height: after choosing a rational basis of the relevant function space, the coefficients of these rational functions may grow too quickly. If coefficient sizes are exponential in \(s\), then a naive box has size roughly \(\exp(O(s^2))\), while the number of directions is only \(\exp(O(s))\), losing the polynomial gain.

The number-field \(S\)-unit approach can be viewed as a way to distribute this denominator/height growth across \(k\) embeddings so that the finite point set still has size only \(\exp(O(k))\).

2.4. Why this is promising, but less than Approach 1

This approach is attractive because:

  1. it uses the elementary parametrization of the circle;
  2. it gives a clear local/CRT target;
  3. it may yield explicit examples if one can write down suitable fields \(K_i\) and controlled lifts.

However, it has serious technical risks:

  • local solutions modulo \(p^E\) do not automatically globalize to exact solutions of \(x^2+y^2=1\);
  • denominator control tends to force \(S\)-unit arguments anyway;
  • one still needs bounded root discriminant or some substitute to keep the point sets of size \(\exp(O(k))\);
  • explicit polynomial families with all the required properties may be difficult to manage.

So I would treat this as a potentially more explicit reformulation of Approach 1, but I would not pursue it before the norm-one \(S\)-unit construction.

Approach 3: Concentric-circle/angular amplification

Summary

Instead of building an additive box in a high-rank group, try to amplify unit distances geometrically using many concentric circles and a finite rotation group.

Let

\[ G_m=\{\zeta^0,\zeta^1,\dots,\zeta^{m-1}\}, \qquad \zeta=e^{2\pi i/m}. \]

Choose radii

\[ R=\{r_1,\dots,r_k\}\subset \mathbb R_{>0} \]

and define

\[ P=\{r_j\zeta^\ell:1\le j\le k,\;0\le \ell<m\}. \]

Then

\[ |P|=km. \]

A pair

\[ r_i\zeta^a,\quad r_j\zeta^b \]

has distance \(1\) exactly when

\[ |r_i-r_j\zeta^{b-a}|=1. \]

Equivalently,

\[ r_i^2+r_j^2-2r_ir_j\cos\left(\frac{2\pi(b-a)}m\right)=1. \]

If for many pairs \((i,j)\) there exists an angular offset \(\ell\) such that

\[ |r_i-r_j\zeta^\ell|=1, \]

then each such triple \((i,j,\ell)\) contributes roughly \(m\) unit distances, one for each global rotation.

Thus

\[ u(P)\approx m\cdot \#\{(i,j,\ell):|r_i-r_j\zeta^\ell|=1\}. \]

If one can arrange

\[ \#\{(i,j,\ell):|r_i-r_j\zeta^\ell|=1\} \ge k^{1+\varepsilon}, \]

and if \(m=k^\alpha\), then

\[ |P|=k^{1+\alpha} \]

and

\[ u(P)\gtrsim k^{1+\alpha+\varepsilon} = |P|^{1+\varepsilon/(1+\alpha)}. \]

So a superlinear graph on the radii, amplified by rotations, would prove the theorem.

3.1. Potential ways to choose the radii

The equation

\[ |r-s\zeta^\ell|=1 \]

is a norm equation:

\[ (r-s\zeta^\ell)(r-s\zeta^{-\ell})=1. \]

Thus one could try to choose radii inside a real subfield of a cyclotomic field, or more generally inside a number field with many controlled norm-one relations.

Possible sources:

(a) Chord lengths in regular polygons

Let

\[ r_a = |1-\xi^a| \]

for some root of unity \(\xi\). Chord lengths satisfy many trigonometric identities, and one can hope to use identities such as Ptolemy relations or Chebyshev recurrences to generate many solutions to

\[ |r_a-r_b\zeta^\ell|=1. \]

The difficulty is that obvious regular-polygon identities usually produce only linear or near-linear numbers of fixed-length relations.

(b) Algebraic units and traces

If \(w\) is a norm-one element, then

\[ w+w^{-1}=2\cos\theta \]

lies in a totally real field. Radii can be chosen as algebraic expressions in such traces. The law of cosines then becomes an algebraic equation in traces of norm-one elements.

This may connect the concentric-circle construction back to norm-one \(S\)-units.

(c) Dense radius graph from a semigroup of transformations

For each angular offset \(\ell\), the equation

\[ r^2+s^2-2rs\cos(2\pi\ell/m)=1 \]

defines a correspondence between \(r\) and \(s\). If one can find a finite set of radii nearly invariant under many such correspondences, the induced graph on radii could have \(k^{1+\varepsilon}\) edges.

This resembles building a finite orbit segment under a collection of algebraic transformations.

3.2. Main obstacle

The amplification only works if the angle offsets come from a small high-multiplicity difference set.

If every radius pair requires its own arbitrary angle, then one does not get the factor \(m\). The reason regular \(m\)-gons are attractive is that each allowed angular offset occurs \(m\) times.

So the real challenge is:

Find many radius pairs whose required angular offsets lie in one common cyclic angular grid.

This is a strong arithmetic constraint. Naive choices of radii give too few good pairs, while arbitrary good angles give no amplification.

3.3. Why this is still worth considering

This approach is geometrically natural and could, in principle, produce cleaner planar configurations. It also gives a direct geometric interpretation of the arithmetic norm equation.

However, compared with Approach 1, it has a harder combinatorial bottleneck: one must build a dense graph on the radii with labels in a small cyclic group.

I would rank this below the number-field/Følner-set approaches, but it is still plausible that a refined arithmetic version of this construction could work.

Ranking

1. High-degree number fields and norm-one \(S\)-units

This is the strongest route. It directly supplies exponentially many unit directions in a rank-\(O(k)\) additive structure, and Minkowski boxes give the needed finite point sets. The proof strategy is highly coherent:

\[ \text{split primes} \Rightarrow \text{many norm-one \(S\)-units} \Rightarrow \text{many unit translations} \Rightarrow u(P)\ge |P|^{1+\delta}. \]

The key technical inputs — bounded-root-discriminant towers with prescribed splitting, class-number bounds, and lattice-point estimates — are standard or at least well-established tools.

Most promising.

2. Parametrized conic / local-to-global construction

This is essentially a coordinate-level version of the first approach. It may yield a more explicit proof using the parametrization

\[ x=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \qquad y=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}. \]

The main task is to construct exponentially many parameters \(t\) with controlled denominators and bounded conjugates. This likely reduces to norm-one \(S\)-units, but the perspective is useful and may simplify parts of the construction.

Second most promising.

3. Concentric-circle/angular amplification

This is the most geometric route. If one can construct many radii \(r_i\) such that many equations

\[ |r_i-r_j\zeta^\ell|=1 \]

hold with \(\ell\) in a common cyclic group, then the rotational symmetry amplifies each radial relation into \(m\) unit distances.

The issue is producing a genuinely dense labeled graph on the radii. Without a strong arithmetic input, this seems difficult. Still, it could potentially be fused with the norm-one \(S\)-unit method.

Third most promising.

We will prove the theorem by carrying out the arithmetic/Følner strategy from the brainstorm.

The proof has four main components.

  1. Construct a tower of totally real fields \(K_i\) of degrees \(k_i\to\infty\), with bounded root discriminant, in which a fixed prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), say \(p=5\), splits completely.
  2. For each \(K_i\), pass to \(L_i=K_i(i)\). Since \(5\) splits completely in \(K_i\) and also in \(\mathbb Q(i)\), there are \(k_i\) independent pairs of conjugate primes in \(L_i\) over \(5\). Using ideal-class pigeonholing, produce exponentially many elements
\[ w\in L_i^\times \]

with

\[ w\overline w=1 \]

and with controlled denominators. Writing

\[ w=a+ib,\qquad a,b\in K_i, \]

we get

\[ a^2+b^2=1. \]

Thus under any real embedding \(\sigma:K_i\hookrightarrow \mathbb R\),

\[ (\sigma(a),\sigma(b)) \]

is a genuine Euclidean unit vector.

  1. Build a large finite set \(B_i\subset D^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}\) by cutting the Minkowski lattice with a fixed box. Because all conjugates of \(a,b\) are bounded by \(1\), translation by \(a\) and \(b\) sends a large inner box into a slightly larger outer box.
  2. Define
\[ P_i=\{(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)):x,y\in B_i\}\subset\mathbb R^2. \]

Every controlled unit vector gives \(\gg |B_i|^2\) directed unit-distance pairs. Since the number of controlled unit vectors is exponential in \(k_i\), while \(|P_i|\) is also exponential in \(k_i\), we get

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for some fixed \(\delta>0\).

We now give the details.

1. A totally real tower in which \(5\) splits completely

We first record a standard class-field-tower consequence of Golod–Shafarevich theory.

Lemma 1: A totally real \(5\)-split unramified tower

There exists a totally real number field \(F\), with root discriminant

\[ \Delta=\operatorname{rd}(F), \]

such that \(5\) splits completely in \(F\), and such that there is an infinite pro-\(2\) extension

\[ \mathcal K/F \]

with the following properties:

  1. \(\mathcal K/F\) is unramified at every finite prime;
  2. every real place of \(F\) splits completely in \(\mathcal K\);
  3. every prime of \(F\) above \(5\) splits completely in \(\mathcal K\).

Consequently, there is an infinite sequence of finite subextensions

\[ F\subset K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots\subset \mathcal K \]

such that, writing

\[ k_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q], \]

we have

\[ k_i\to\infty, \]

each \(K_i\) is totally real,

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)=\Delta, \]

and \(5\) splits completely in \(K_i\).

Proof of Lemma 1

Choose \(t=30\) distinct rational primes

\[ q_1,\dots,q_t\equiv 1\pmod {20}, \]

and put

\[ Q=q_1q_2\cdots q_t,\qquad F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt Q). \]

Then \(F\) is real quadratic. Since \(Q\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the discriminant of \(F\) is \(Q\). Also, since every \(q_j\equiv 1\pmod 5\), we have \(Q\equiv 1\pmod 5\), so \(5\) splits in \(F\). Let

\[ T=\{\mathfrak t_1,\mathfrak t_2\} \]

be the two primes of \(F\) above \(5\).

Let \(G\) be the Galois group over \(F\) of the maximal pro-\(2\) extension of \(F\) which is

  • unramified at all finite primes;
  • split at the two real places of \(F\);
  • split at the two primes \(\mathfrak t_1,\mathfrak t_2\).

We claim \(G\) is infinite.

Let \(d=d(G)\) and \(r=r(G)\) denote the generator rank and relation rank of \(G\).

By genus theory, the narrow class group of the real quadratic field \(F\) has \(2\)-rank \(t-1\). Passing from the narrow to the ordinary class group can reduce the \(2\)-rank by at most \(1\), so the ordinary class group has \(2\)-rank at least

\[ t-2. \]

Forcing the two primes in \(T\) to split amounts, on the maximal abelian unramified quotient, to quotienting by the subgroup generated by their ideal classes. This lowers the \(2\)-rank by at most \(2\). Hence

\[ d\ge t-4=26. \]

On the other hand, Shafarevich's relation-rank estimate for such a \(T\)-split unramified pro-\(2\) group gives

\[ r\le d+r_1(F)+r_2(F)+|T|+1. \]

Here \(F\) is real quadratic, so \(r_1(F)=2\), \(r_2(F)=0\), and \(|T|=2\). Thus

\[ r\le d+5. \]

If \(G\) were finite, the Golod–Shafarevich inequality would imply

\[ r>\frac{d^2}{4}. \]

But for every \(d\ge 26\),

\[ d+5<\frac{d^2}{4}. \]

This is impossible. Hence \(G\) is infinite.

Choose open normal subgroups \(G_i\subset G\) with indices tending to infinity, and let

\[ K_i=\mathcal K^{G_i}. \]

Then \([K_i:F]\to\infty\), hence \(k_i=[K_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty\). Since \(\mathcal K/F\) is unramified at finite primes, each \(K_i/F\) is unramified, so

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)=\operatorname{rd}(F)=:\Delta. \]

Since all real places split, every \(K_i\) is totally real. Since the two primes of \(F\) above \(5\) split completely in \(\mathcal K\), the rational prime \(5\) splits completely in every \(K_i\).

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

2. A uniform class-number bound

We will need a crude but uniform bound for the class numbers of the fields

\[ L_i=K_i(i). \]

The following elementary estimate suffices.

Lemma 2: Exponential class-number bound at bounded root discriminant

For every \(R\ge 1\), there is a constant \(C_{\mathrm{cl}}(R)\) such that every number field \(M\) of degree \(n\) and root discriminant at most \(R\) satisfies

\[ h(M)\le C_{\mathrm{cl}}(R)^n. \]

\[ (w_{\mathbf e}) = \prod_{j=1}^k \left( \frac{\mathfrak P_j}{\overline{\mathfrak P}_j} \right)^{2(e_j-e_j^{(0)})}. \]

Therefore every valuation of \(w_{\mathbf e}\) above \(5\) lies between \(-2E\) and \(2E\), and \(w_{\mathbf e}\) has no other nonzero valuations. Hence

\[ 5^{2E}w_{\mathbf e}\in\mathcal O_L, \qquad 5^{2E}\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}\in\mathcal O_L. \]

Write

\[ a_{\mathbf e} = \frac{w_{\mathbf e}+\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}}{2}, \qquad b_{\mathbf e} = \frac{w_{\mathbf e}-\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}}{2i}. \]

Then \(a_{\mathbf e},b_{\mathbf e}\in K\). Also,

\[ M a_{\mathbf e} = 2\cdot 5^{2E} \cdot \frac{w_{\mathbf e}+\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}}{2} = 5^{2E}(w_{\mathbf e}+\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}) \in \mathcal O_L\cap K=\mathcal O_K. \]

Similarly,

\[ M b_{\mathbf e} = 5^{2E}\frac{w_{\mathbf e}-\overline{w_{\mathbf e}}}{i} \in \mathcal O_L\cap K=\mathcal O_K, \]

since \(i\) is a unit of \(\mathcal O_L\). Thus

\[ a_{\mathbf e},b_{\mathbf e}\in M^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Finally,

\[ w_{\mathbf e}=a_{\mathbf e}+ib_{\mathbf e}, \]

so

\[ 1=w_{\mathbf e}\overline{w_{\mathbf e}} = (a_{\mathbf e}+ib_{\mathbf e})(a_{\mathbf e}-ib_{\mathbf e}) = a_{\mathbf e}^2+b_{\mathbf e}^2. \]

Thus every pair \((a_{\mathbf e},b_{\mathbf e})\) satisfies

\[ a_{\mathbf e}^2+b_{\mathbf e}^2=1. \]

The pairs \((a_{\mathbf e},b_{\mathbf e})\) are distinct. Indeed, if

\[ (a_{\mathbf e},b_{\mathbf e})=(a_{\mathbf f},b_{\mathbf f}), \]

then \(w_{\mathbf e}=w_{\mathbf f}\), so \((w_{\mathbf e})=(w_{\mathbf f})\). Comparing valuations at \(\mathfrak P_j\) gives

\[ 2(e_j-e_j^{(0)})=2(f_j-e_j^{(0)}) \]

for every \(j\), hence \(\mathbf e=\mathbf f\).

Therefore we have constructed a set

\[ \mathcal U_K\subset (M^{-1}\mathcal O_K)^2 \]

such that

\[ |\mathcal U_K|\ge A^k \]

and every \((a,b)\in\mathcal U_K\) satisfies

\[ a^2+b^2=1. \]

Consequently, for every real embedding \(\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\),

\[ \tau(a)^2+\tau(b)^2=1, \]

so

\[ |\tau(a)|\le 1,\qquad |\tau(b)|\le 1. \]

5. Minkowski boxes

Let

\[ \tau_1,\dots,\tau_k:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb R \]

be the real embeddings of \(K\), and let

\[ \Phi:K\to\mathbb R^k \]

be the Minkowski embedding

\[ \Phi(x)=(\tau_1(x),\dots,\tau_k(x)). \]

Set

\[ \Lambda=M^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Since \(K\) is totally real and has root discriminant \(\Delta\),

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi(\mathcal O_K)) = \sqrt{|\operatorname{disc}(K)|} = \Delta^{k/2}. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Phi(\Lambda)) = \left(\frac{\sqrt\Delta}{M}\right)^k. \]

For \(\rho>0\) and \(\mathbf t\in\mathbb R^k\), define

\[ B_\rho(\mathbf t) = \{x\in\Lambda:\Phi(x)\in \mathbf t+[-\rho,\rho]^k\}. \]

Averaging over a fundamental domain of \(\Phi(\Lambda)\),

\[ \mathbb E_{\mathbf t}|B_2(\mathbf t)| = \frac{\operatorname{vol}([-2,2]^k)} {\operatorname{covol}(\Phi(\Lambda))} = \frac{4^k}{(\sqrt\Delta/M)^k} = C_{\mathrm{in}}^k. \]

Hence there exists \(\mathbf t=\mathbf t_K\) such that

\[ |B_2(\mathbf t_K)|\ge C_{\mathrm{in}}^k. \]

Fix such a translate and write

\[ B_{\mathrm{in}}=B_2(\mathbf t_K), \qquad B_{\mathrm{out}}=B_3(\mathbf t_K). \]

Then

\[ |B_{\mathrm{in}}|\ge C_{\mathrm{in}}^k. \]

We next upper-bound \(B_{\mathrm{out}}\). If \(x,y\in\Lambda\) and \(x\ne y\), then

\[ M(x-y)\in\mathcal O_K\setminus\{0\}. \]

Thus

\[ \left|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(M(x-y))\right|\ge 1. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \prod_{j=1}^k |\tau_j(x-y)|\ge M^{-k}. \]

Therefore

\[ \max_j |\tau_j(x-y)|\ge M^{-1}. \]

So the points of \(\Phi(\Lambda)\) are separated in \(\ell^\infty\)-distance by at least \(M^{-1}\).

The \(\ell^\infty\)-cubes of radius \(1/(2M)\) centered at the points of \(\Phi(B_{\mathrm{out}})\) have disjoint interiors and all lie inside a cube of side length

\[ 6+\frac1M. \]

Each small cube has volume \(M^{-k}\). Therefore

\[ |B_{\mathrm{out}}|M^{-k} \le \left(6+\frac1M\right)^k, \]

so

\[ |B_{\mathrm{out}}| \le (6M+1)^k = C_{\mathrm{out}}^k. \]

Finally, if \(c\in M^{-1}\mathcal O_K\) satisfies

\[ |\tau_j(c)|\le 1 \]

for all \(j\), then

\[ B_{\mathrm{in}}+c\subseteq B_{\mathrm{out}}, \]

because the inner box has radius \(2\), the outer box has radius \(3\), and every conjugate of \(c\) has absolute value at most \(1\).

6. The planar point set

Use the first real embedding

\[ \sigma=\tau_1. \]

Define

\[ P_K = \{(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)):x,y\in B_{\mathrm{out}}\} \subset\mathbb R^2. \]

Since \(\sigma\) is injective on \(K\), the map

\[ B_{\mathrm{out}}\times B_{\mathrm{out}} \to P_K, \qquad (x,y)\mapsto(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)) \]

is injective. Hence

\[ |P_K|=|B_{\mathrm{out}}|^2. \]

Therefore

\[ |P_K|\le C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k}. \]

Also \(B_{\mathrm{in}}\subseteq B_{\mathrm{out}}\), so

\[ |P_K|\ge |B_{\mathrm{in}}|^2\ge C_{\mathrm{in}}^{2k}. \]

Since \(C_{\mathrm{in}}>1\) and \(k=k_i\to\infty\), we will have

\[ |P_{K_i}|\to\infty. \]

7. Counting unit distances

Let \((a,b)\in\mathcal U_K\). Then

\[ a,b\in M^{-1}\mathcal O_K, \]

and for every embedding \(\tau_j\),

\[ |\tau_j(a)|\le 1,\qquad |\tau_j(b)|\le 1. \]

Thus

\[ B_{\mathrm{in}}+a\subseteq B_{\mathrm{out}}, \qquad B_{\mathrm{in}}+b\subseteq B_{\mathrm{out}}. \]

For every \(x,y\in B_{\mathrm{in}}\), define

\[ p=(\sigma(x),\sigma(y)), \qquad q=(\sigma(x+a),\sigma(y+b)). \]

Then \(p,q\in P_K\). Moreover,

\[ \|p-q\|_2^2 = \sigma(a)^2+\sigma(b)^2 = \sigma(a^2+b^2) = \sigma(1) = 1. \]

So \((p,q)\) is a directed unit-distance pair.

The pairs constructed in this way are all distinct. Indeed, if two quadruples \((a,b,x,y)\) and \((a',b',x',y')\) produce the same ordered pair \((p,q)\), then equality of the first coordinates gives

\[ \sigma(x)=\sigma(x'), \qquad \sigma(y)=\sigma(y'), \]

so \(x=x'\) and \(y=y'\). Equality of the second points then gives

\[ \sigma(a)=\sigma(a'), \qquad \sigma(b)=\sigma(b'), \]

so \(a=a'\) and \(b=b'\). Thus no overcounting occurs.

Therefore

\[ |D(P_K)| \ge |\mathcal U_K|\,|B_{\mathrm{in}}|^2 \ge A^k C_{\mathrm{in}}^{2k}. \]

Hence

\[ u(P_K) = \frac12 |D(P_K)| \ge \frac12 A^k C_{\mathrm{in}}^{2k}. \]

By definition of \(\Theta\),

\[ A C_{\mathrm{in}}^2 = \Theta C_{\mathrm{out}}^2. \]

Thus

\[ u(P_K) \ge \frac12 \Theta^k C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k}. \]

On the other hand,

\[ |P_K|\le C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k}. \]

Therefore

\[ |P_K|^{1+\delta} \le C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k(1+\delta)} = C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k} \left(C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2\delta}\right)^k. \]

Since

\[ \delta= \frac{\log\Theta}{4\log C_{\mathrm{out}}}, \]

we have

\[ C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2\delta} = \Theta^{1/2}. \]

Thus

\[ |P_K|^{1+\delta} \le C_{\mathrm{out}}^{2k}\Theta^{k/2}. \]

Because \(\Theta>4\), we have

\[ \frac12\Theta^k\ge \Theta^{k/2} \]

for every \(k\ge 1\). Hence

\[ u(P_K)\ge |P_K|^{1+\delta}. \]

Applying this construction to \(K=K_i\), define

\[ P_i=P_{K_i}. \]

Then

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for all \(i\). This proves the theorem. \(\square\)
