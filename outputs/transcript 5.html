Theorem. There exist an absolute constant $\delta > 0$ and an infinite sequence $\{P_i\}$ of finite point sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $|P_i| \to \infty$ and $u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}$ for all $i$, where $u(P_i) = \frac{1}{2} | \{ (p,q) \in P_i \times P_i : \|p-q\|_2 = 1 \} |$ is the number of unit-distance pairs.

Assistant

I’ll treat the theorem as a construction problem. The basic identity to keep in mind is

\[ 2u(P)=\sum_{\substack{v\in \mathbb R^2\\ \|v\|=1}} |P\cap(P-v)|. \]

So a very efficient way to prove the theorem is to build a finite set \(P\) together with many unit vectors \(v\) such that translation by \(v\) preserves a large fraction of \(P\). If we can arrange

\[ |\{v:\|v\|=1,\ |P\cap(P-v)|\gg |P|\}|\ge |P|^\delta, \]

then immediately \(u(P)\gg |P|^{1+\delta}\).

Below are the three approaches I would regard as the most promising. I rank them at the end.

1. High-degree algebraic-number / norm-form construction

Core idea

Generalize Erdős’s classical lattice construction from \(\mathbb Z[i]\), but instead of trying to make one ordinary integer have many representations as a sum of two squares, pass to number fields of increasing degree where a fixed integer has exponentially many prime-ideal factorizations. The exponential growth in the field degree can then become a fixed power of the number of constructed points.

This is the approach I would try first.

The guiding template is:

  1. Choose a sequence of CM fields \(K_i/F_i\), where \(F_i\) is totally real, \(K_i\) is totally imaginary, and

    \[ d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\to \infty. \]

  2. Fix one complex embedding

    \[ \sigma_i:K_i\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\simeq \mathbb R^2. \]

  3. Find many algebraic integers \(\alpha\in \mathcal O_{K_i}\) satisfying a relative norm equation

    \[ \alpha\overline{\alpha}=B \]

    for some fixed positive integer \(B\), independent of \(i\).

  4. Then for every such \(\alpha\),

    \[ \left|\frac{\sigma_i(\alpha)}{\sqrt B}\right|=1. \]

    Thus each \(\alpha\) gives a unit translation vector in the complex plane.

  5. Choose a large finite chunk \(X_i\subset \mathcal O_{K_i}\), and project it to the plane by

    \[ P_i=\left\{\frac{\sigma_i(x)}{\sqrt B}:x\in X_i\right\}. \]

    If \(X_i\) is chosen so that for many \(\alpha\)’s, many \(x\in X_i\) also satisfy \(x+\alpha\in X_i\), then every such pair gives a unit distance.

The whole problem becomes: produce exponentially many solutions to \(\alpha\bar\alpha=B\) in fields of increasing degree, while keeping the additive chunks \(X_i\) only exponentially large in the degree.

The key arithmetic target lemma

A clean target lemma would be the following.

Target Lemma A

There exist constants \(A>1\), \(B\in \mathbb Z_{>0}\), and a sequence of CM fields \(K_i/F_i\) with \(d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\to \infty\), such that:

  1. the root discriminants of \(K_i\) are uniformly bounded:

    \[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)=|\operatorname{Disc}(K_i)|^{1/[K_i:\mathbb Q]}\le C; \]

  2. the relative norm equation

    \[ \alpha\bar\alpha=B \]

    has at least \(A^{d_i}\) distinct solutions \(\alpha\in \mathcal O_{K_i}\).

If this lemma is available, then the geometric part is almost automatic.

Why Target Lemma A would imply the theorem

Let

\[ S_i=\{\alpha\in\mathcal O_{K_i}:\alpha\bar\alpha=B\}. \]

Assume \(|S_i|\ge A^{d_i}\).

Fix \(T>\sqrt B\), to be chosen large but independent of \(i\). Define an archimedean box

\[ X_i(T)=\{x\in \mathcal O_{K_i}: |\tau(x)|\le T\text{ for every complex embedding }\tau:K_i\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\}. \]

Then define

\[ P_i(T)=\left\{\frac{\sigma_i(x)}{\sqrt B}:x\in X_i(T)\right\}\subset \mathbb C\simeq \mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\sigma_i\) is injective on \(K_i\), distinct algebraic integers give distinct planar points.

Now take \(\alpha\in S_i\). Since \(\alpha\bar\alpha=B\), for every embedding \(\tau\),

\[ |\tau(\alpha)|=\sqrt B. \]

Therefore, if \(x\in X_i(T-\sqrt B)\), then

\[ |\tau(x+\alpha)|\le |\tau(x)|+|\tau(\alpha)|\le T \]

for every \(\tau\). Hence

\[ x\in X_i(T-\sqrt B)\implies x+\alpha\in X_i(T). \]

For each such \(x\), the two planar points

\[ \frac{\sigma_i(x)}{\sqrt B},\qquad \frac{\sigma_i(x+\alpha)}{\sqrt B} \]

are at Euclidean distance

\[ \left|\frac{\sigma_i(\alpha)}{\sqrt B}\right|=1. \]

Thus the number of directed unit-distance pairs is at least

\[ |S_i|\,|X_i(T-\sqrt B)|. \]

After dividing by \(2\), this gives

\[ u(P_i(T))\ge \frac12 |S_i|\,|X_i(T-\sqrt B)|. \]

Now use geometry of numbers. Since the root discriminants are bounded, the lattice \(\mathcal O_{K_i}\) in its Minkowski embedding has covolume only exponentially large in \(d_i\), and the volume of the archimedean box is also exponential in \(d_i\). Thus for fixed \(T\) there are constants \(C_T,c_T>1\) such that

\[ |X_i(T)|\asymp C_T^{d_i}, \]

and

\[ |X_i(T-\sqrt B)|\ge \rho_T^{d_i}|X_i(T)| \]

for some \(\rho_T\in(0,1)\) satisfying \(\rho_T\to 1\) as \(T\to\infty\).

Consequently,

\[ u(P_i(T)) \ge \frac12 A^{d_i}\rho_T^{d_i}|X_i(T)| =\frac12 (A\rho_T)^{d_i}|P_i(T)|. \]

Choose \(T\) large enough that

\[ A\rho_T>1. \]

Since

\[ |P_i(T)|=|X_i(T)|\asymp C_T^{d_i}, \]

we get

\[ (A\rho_T)^{d_i} = |P_i(T)|^{\delta+o(1)} \]

with

\[ \delta=\frac{\log(A\rho_T)}{\log C_T}>0. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_i(T))\ge |P_i(T)|^{1+\delta-o(1)}. \]

By slightly adjusting constants, or passing to a subsequence, this gives the theorem.

So the entire construction reduces to proving Target Lemma A.

How to prove Target Lemma A

This is where class field theory should enter.

The known obstruction in \(\mathbb Z[i]\) is that the number of representations of an integer \(m\) as \(a^2+b^2\) is essentially a divisor-counting function, and divisor functions grow only as \(m^{o(1)}\). The escape route is to fix \(m=B\) but let the number field degree grow. In a degree-\(d\) field, a fixed rational prime can split into \(d\) prime ideals. If each of those prime ideals further splits in a CM extension, then one can choose one of two conjugate prime ideals independently at each of \(d\) places, giving \(2^d\) possible ideal factorizations.

Concretely:

  1. Pick rational primes

    \[ p_1,\dots,p_s\equiv 1\pmod 4. \]

  2. Let

    \[ B=p_1p_2\cdots p_s. \]
  3. Construct totally real fields \(F_i\) of degree \(d_i\to\infty\), bounded root discriminant, in which all the \(p_j\)’s split completely.

  4. Let

    \[ K_i=F_i(i). \]

  5. Since \(p_j\equiv 1\pmod 4\), primes above \(p_j\) in \(F_i\) split in \(K_i/F_i\). For each prime \(\mathfrak p\mid p_j\) of \(F_i\), there are two conjugate primes

    \[ \mathfrak P,\overline{\mathfrak P} \]

    above \(\mathfrak p\) in \(K_i\).

  6. Choosing one of \(\mathfrak P,\overline{\mathfrak P}\) for every prime \(\mathfrak p\mid p_j\) and every \(j\) produces about

    \[ 2^{s d_i} \]

    possible relative norm factorizations.

  7. The main technical issue is to turn these ideal factorizations into principal ideals generated by elements \(\alpha\) satisfying

    \[ \alpha\bar\alpha=B. \]

This is where one likely uses Hilbert class fields, ray class fields, principalization, or a carefully designed class-field tower. The bounded-root-discriminant condition is natural because unramified extensions preserve root discriminant. One would want a tower in which the chosen primes split completely and the relevant ideals principalize.

There may be a unit-normalization issue: a generator might satisfy

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=B\varepsilon \]

for a totally positive unit \(\varepsilon\in \mathcal O_{F_i}^\times\). One then needs to divide by a relative norm-one unit or choose generators more carefully so that the unit factor disappears. This is a serious but standard-looking CM/class-field-theoretic problem.

Why this route looks extremely promising

This approach directly attacks the weakness of the old Erdős construction.

In \(\mathbb Z[i]\), to get many unit directions one chooses \(m\) with many Gaussian divisors. But the number of divisors of \(m\) is only \(m^{o(1)}\), leading to the classical lower bound \(n^{1+o(1)}\).

In the high-degree construction, one instead fixes \(B\) and obtains exponentially many factorizations because \(B\) splits into linearly many prime ideals as the degree grows. The point set size is also exponential in the degree, so the ratio becomes a fixed power.

The geometry is clean: every solution \(\alpha\bar\alpha=B\) gives an exact unit vector after scaling by \(\sqrt B\). No approximation is involved.

This is my top-ranked approach.

2. Periodic quotient / flat-torus amplification, guided by finite-field unit graphs

Core idea

Work first on a compact quotient of the plane, such as a flat torus

\[ T=\mathbb R^2/\Lambda. \]

On the torus, define a “unit-difference graph” on a finite set \(B\subset T\) by joining \(b,b'\in B\) if their difference is represented by an honest Euclidean unit vector in \(\mathbb R^2\). If one can find a finite set \(B\subset T\) with many such edges, then one can unfold many copies of \(B\) into the plane, converting torus edges into genuine Euclidean unit distances.

This is a powerful amplification mechanism because torus wrap-around can simulate modular or finite-field phenomena, but after unfolding, all distances are honest Euclidean distances.

The torus-to-plane unfolding lemma

Let \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^2\) be a lattice and let

\[ \pi:\mathbb R^2\to T=\mathbb R^2/\Lambda \]

be the quotient map.

For \(B\subset T\), define its torus unit graph \(G_T(B)\) by putting an edge between \(b,b'\in B\) if there exist representatives \(\tilde b,\tilde b'\in \mathbb R^2\) and some \(\lambda\in\Lambda\) such that

\[ \|\tilde b'-\tilde b+\lambda\|_2=1. \]

Suppose \(G_T(B)\) has \(k\) vertices and \(e\) edges.

Choose representatives \(\tilde b\in\mathbb R^2\) for \(b\in B\). For each torus edge \(b b'\), choose one lattice vector \(\lambda_{bb'}\in\Lambda\) such that

\[ \|\tilde b'-\tilde b+\lambda_{bb'}\|_2=1. \]

Now choose a large finite patch \(F\subset\Lambda\), for instance

\[ F_R=\{m\lambda_1+n\lambda_2: |m|,|n|\le R\}. \]

Define the unfolded planar point set

\[ P=\{\tilde b+\mu:b\in B,\ \mu\in F_R\}. \]

Then

\[ |P|=k|F_R|. \]

For every torus edge \(bb'\), and for every \(\mu\in F_R\) such that \(\mu+\lambda_{bb'}\in F_R\), the two points

\[ \tilde b+\mu,\qquad \tilde b'+\mu+\lambda_{bb'} \]

are at Euclidean distance \(1\).

If all edge-labels \(\lambda_{bb'}\) have coordinates of size at most \(L\) in the lattice basis and \(R\gg L\), then for most \(\mu\in F_R\), both \(\mu\) and \(\mu+\lambda_{bb'}\) remain in \(F_R\). Hence

\[ u(P)\gtrsim e|F_R|. \]

Thus, if one can build torus configurations with

\[ e\ge k^{1+\eta} \]

for some fixed \(\eta>0\), and with edge labels not too large, then unfolding yields planar point sets with superlinear unit distances.

For example, if \(R\le k^C\), then

\[ |P|=k|F_R|\le k^{1+2C}, \]

while

\[ u(P)\gtrsim k^{1+\eta}|F_R|. \]

This gives

\[ u(P)\gtrsim |P|^{1+\delta} \]

with

\[ \delta\approx \frac{\eta}{1+2C}. \]

So the key target becomes:

Target Lemma B

There exists \(\eta>0\) and a sequence of flat tori \(T_i=\mathbb R^2/\Lambda_i\) with finite sets \(B_i\subset T_i\) such that

\[ e(G_{T_i}(B_i))\ge |B_i|^{1+\eta}, \]

and the corresponding lattice labels needed to realize those torus edges have polynomially bounded size.

Why finite fields are a natural guide

Over a finite field \(\mathbb F_q\), the unit-distance graph on \(\mathbb F_q^2\), with adjacency

\[ (x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2=1, \]

has approximately

\[ q^2 \]

vertices and degree roughly

\[ q, \]

so it has approximately

\[ q^3 \]

edges. In terms of the number of vertices \(n=q^2\), this is

\[ n^{3/2}. \]

Of course, one cannot simply identify \(\mathbb F_q^2\) with a subset of \(\mathbb R^2\) and preserve the equation exactly. But finite-field unit graphs are valuable templates:

  1. They are dense.
  2. They satisfy the same basic local obstruction as real unit-distance graphs: two vertices have at most two common unit neighbors.
  3. They can be regarded as modular or quotient phenomena, which is philosophically close to working on a flat torus.

So one possible plan is:

  1. Start with a dense finite-field unit graph.
  2. Extract a large subgraph with controlled cycle structure or controlled labels.
  3. Realize that subgraph as a torus unit graph.
  4. Unfold the torus configuration into the Euclidean plane.

The torus stage is crucial. It allows cycle sums to close only modulo \(\Lambda\), avoiding some rigidity obstacles that would appear if one tried to realize the finite-field graph directly in the plane.

A more geometric way to build dense torus unit graphs

Let \(\Lambda\) be very small. The image of the Euclidean unit circle

\[ S^1=\{x\in\mathbb R^2:\|x\|=1\} \]

under the quotient map

\[ \pi:\mathbb R^2\to \mathbb R^2/\Lambda \]

wraps around the torus many times. The torus unit graph is the Cayley-type graph whose allowed differences lie in

\[ \pi(S^1)\subset T. \]

If \(\pi(S^1)\) has many branches or self-intersections, then one might try to place \(B\) on a structured set of intersection points so that many pairwise differences land in \(\pi(S^1)\).

This becomes an incidence problem on a torus:

\[ b'\in b+\pi(S^1). \]

So one seeks many incidences between a finite set \(B\) and translates of the wrapped circle \(\pi(S^1)\).

This is reminiscent of point-circle incidence geometry, except the quotient topology creates many wraps, which may allow denser configurations than are easy to see directly in the plane. Once found, the unfolding lemma turns them into ordinary unit distances.

Main obstacles

The main obstacles are:

  1. Exactness. Approximate torus unit differences are useless; one needs exact representatives of length \(1\).

  2. Label control. If the lattice vectors \(\lambda_{bb'}\) needed to realize the torus edges are enormous, the unfolding patch \(F_R\) must be enormous, and the exponent gain may disappear.

  3. Rigidity/cycle consistency. Dense abstract graphs generally cannot be realized as unit-distance graphs, even on a torus. One needs a structured family of graphs whose edge constraints are algebraically dependent.

  4. Avoiding forbidden complete bipartite subgraphs. In the plane, two points have at most two common unit neighbors, so \(K_{2,3}\) is impossible. Any torus construction intended for unfolding must respect the analogous local behavior.

Why this route is still promising

The torus unfolding lemma is very clean. If one can prove Target Lemma B, the theorem follows with little additional work.

This route also explains how one might evade the common-denominator bottleneck in rational constructions. Instead of forcing all unit directions to live in one enormous planar lattice, one lets directions close modulo a lattice and only later unfolds a finite patch.

I would rank this second: very powerful if the dense torus configurations can be found, but the construction of those configurations is subtler than in the norm-form approach.

3. Cartesian-product / popular-difference construction

Core idea

Look for point sets of product type

\[ P=A\times B\subset \mathbb R^2. \]

Then unit distances can be counted in terms of differences in \(A\) and \(B\). Define the difference multiplicity function

\[ r_A(x)=|\{(a,a')\in A^2:a-a'=x\}| \]

and similarly for \(B\). Then the number of directed unit-distance pairs in \(A\times B\) is

\[ \sum_{\substack{x,y\in\mathbb R\\ x^2+y^2=1}} r_A(x)r_B(y). \]

Thus

\[ 2u(A\times B) = \sum_{\substack{x^2+y^2=1}} r_A(x)r_B(y). \]

So we want many points \((x,y)\) on the unit circle such that \(x\) is a popular difference of \(A\) and \(y\) is a popular difference of \(B\).

This turns the problem into an additive-combinatorial one: construct finite one-dimensional sets \(A,B\) whose popular difference sets contain many coordinate projections of unit-circle points.

Parameter target

Suppose

\[ |A|=|B|=N. \]

Then

\[ |P|=N^2. \]

To prove

\[ u(P)\ge |P|^{1+\delta}=N^{2+2\delta}, \]

it suffices to find \(M\) unit-circle points \((x_j,y_j)\) such that

\[ r_A(x_j)\ge T,\qquad r_B(y_j)\ge T \]

for all \(j=1,\dots,M\), with

\[ MT^2\ge N^{2+2\delta}. \]

The largest possible \(T\) is about \(N\), as in an arithmetic progression. If one could achieve \(T\asymp N\), then it would be enough to find

\[ M\ge N^{2\delta} \]

unit-circle directions whose two coordinates are popular differences.

The classical integer-lattice construction corresponds roughly to the case where \(A=B\) is an arithmetic progression after scaling. Then the relevant unit-circle points correspond to representations

\[ a^2+b^2=m. \]

The number of such representations is \(m^{o(1)}\), which yields only \(n^{1+o(1)}\). Therefore this approach needs a more flexible choice of \(A,B\) than ordinary arithmetic progressions.

Difference-basis strategy

A useful subproblem is the following.

Target Lemma C

Find finite sets \(A,B\subset\mathbb R\) of size \(N\), and a set

\[ \mathcal V\subset S^1 \]

with \(|\mathcal V|\ge N^\varepsilon\), such that for every \((x,y)\in\mathcal V\),

\[ r_A(x)\gg N^c,\qquad r_B(y)\gg N^c \]

for some fixed \(c>0\).

Then

\[ u(A\times B) \gg |\mathcal V|N^{2c}. \]

If

\[ \varepsilon+2c>2, \]

this gives a superlinear bound in \(|A\times B|=N^2\).

One way to build popular differences is as follows. Suppose \(X\subset\mathbb R\) is a desired set of differences. If \(U\) is a long interval or progression, and

\[ A=U\cup(U+X), \]

then every \(x\in X\) occurs as a difference at least \(|U|\) times. But this is efficient only if \(U+X\) is not too large. Thus one wants the coordinate set \(X\) of many unit-circle points to have small additive expansion with a large progression \(U\).

So the real problem is:

Find many points \((x,y)\in S^1\) such that the set of \(x\)-coordinates and the set of \(y\)-coordinates are both additively structured.

This is a highly concrete additive-combinatorial version of the theorem.

Rational parametrization of the circle

The unit circle has the rational parametrization

\[ x(t)=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad y(t)=\frac{2t}{1+t^2}. \]

So another version of Target Lemma C is:

Find a large set \(T\subset\mathbb R\) such that both

\[ X=\left\{\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}:t\in T\right\} \]

and

\[ Y=\left\{\frac{2t}{1+t^2}:t\in T\right\} \]

are contained in popular difference sets of small one-dimensional sets \(A,B\).

Naively taking \(t=a/b\) with bounded denominator creates many rational unit-circle points, but their denominators vary. Forcing them into one common grid usually introduces a huge least common multiple, wiping out the gain. Therefore one needs a more subtle construction, perhaps using smooth denominators, multiplicative subgroups, or number-field parametrizations where many denominators share controlled factors.

Parallel-line version

A closely related but sometimes more flexible variant is to take \(P\) as a union of many horizontal arithmetic progressions:

\[ P=\bigcup_{j=1}^L \bigl((X+s_j)\times\{y_j\}\bigr), \]

where \(X\subset\mathbb R\) is a long progression and each line has a horizontal shift \(s_j\).

For two lines \(i,j\), with vertical separation

\[ h_{ij}=y_j-y_i, \]

a unit distance between the two lines requires horizontal offset

\[ \pm \sqrt{1-h_{ij}^2}. \]

Because the two copies of \(X\) are shifted by \(s_i,s_j\), the relevant condition is

\[ s_j-s_i + x'-x = \pm\sqrt{1-h_{ij}^2} \]

for many pairs \(x,x'\in X\).

If \(X\) is an interval of length \(N\), and for many line pairs \((i,j)\) the required offset lies in \(X-X\) with high multiplicity, then each good line pair contributes \(\asymp N\) unit distances.

Thus if the index set

\[ Q=\{(s_j,y_j):1\le j\le L\} \]

has \(L^{1+\eta}\) good pairs, then the resulting planar set has roughly

\[ |P|=NL \]

points and

\[ u(P)\gtrsim N L^{1+\eta} \]

unit distances. If \(N\) is polynomially bounded in \(L\), this yields a positive exponent.

This reduces the theorem to building a dense graph on the line-index parameters \((s_j,y_j)\), where edges are allowed if the corresponding unit offset is compatible with the difference set \(X-X\). This is like a one-dimensional amplification of a smaller unit-distance-like problem.

Why this route is attractive

This approach has several advantages:

  1. It is elementary and explicit.
  2. It reduces the problem to concrete additive-combinatorial quantities.
  3. It is well suited to computational experimentation.
  4. It can potentially yield very clean point sets, such as Cartesian products or unions of parallel lines.

One can search for large finite sets \(A,B\) maximizing the “circle energy”

\[ E_\circ(A,B) = \sum_{x^2+y^2=1} r_A(x)r_B(y). \]

If one finds \(E_\circ(A,B)\ge (|A||B|)^{1+\delta}\), then \(P=A\times B\) proves the theorem.

Main obstacles

The reason I rank this below the previous two approaches is that the most obvious choices fall back into known divisor-counting limitations.

For example:

  • If \(A,B\) are arithmetic progressions, the problem reduces to representations by a binary quadratic form.
  • If one uses rational points on the circle with many different denominators, the common-denominator size can explode.
  • Sum-product phenomena suggest that a large subset of the unit circle should not have both coordinate projections too additively structured, unless there is a hidden algebraic reason.

Still, this approach remains valuable. It may be that the accepted construction has a product-set shadow, and proving the needed additive lemma directly could yield a relatively elementary proof.

Ranking

1. Most promising: high-degree norm-form construction

This is the most convincing route because it gives a direct mechanism for producing many exact unit vectors. The desired exponent comes from comparing two exponential quantities in the field degree:

  • exponentially many solutions to \(\alpha\bar\alpha=B\);
  • exponentially many points in an additive chunk of \(\mathcal O_K\).

The core construction is also a natural high-degree upgrade of Erdős’s original Gaussian-integer lattice idea. The main burden is arithmetic: prove the required class-field-theoretic norm-solution lemma while keeping root discriminants and unit factors controlled.

I would pursue this first.

2. Second most promising: torus / finite-field quotient amplification

This approach has a clean geometric amplification lemma: dense torus unit graphs unfold into genuine planar unit-distance constructions. Finite-field unit graphs provide dense templates, and the torus setting may absorb modular cycle inconsistencies that would obstruct a direct planar realization.

The main challenge is constructing dense torus configurations with exact unit differences and controlled lattice labels. If that can be done, the rest of the proof is straightforward.

3. Third most promising: Cartesian-product / popular-difference method

This is the most elementary and computationally accessible approach. It reduces the theorem to finding large one-dimensional sets \(A,B\) with high circle energy

\[ \sum_{x^2+y^2=1} r_A(x)r_B(y). \]

However, the naive versions are blocked by classical divisor bounds and common-denominator growth. It likely needs a genuinely new additive-combinatorial insight, perhaps one secretly related to the number-field construction in Approach 1.

So I would keep it as a useful parallel track, but I would not make it the primary route.

Assistant

Below I develop the high-degree norm-form approach into a full proof. The proof is somewhat different from the most naive version sketched earlier: instead of insisting that every ideal-choice be principal, we take many ideal-choices in the same ideal class and divide them. The quotients are principal automatically and have relative norm a unit. After one more pigeonhole over the unit-norm quotient, we obtain exponentially many translation vectors whose relative norm is exactly the same totally positive unit. Anisotropic boxes in the Minkowski embedding then turn these translation vectors into genuine unit distances in one chosen complex embedding.

I will identify \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\).

1. A splitting tower of totally real fields

We need a sequence of totally real fields of growing degree, bounded root discriminant, in which a fixed rational prime splits completely.

We use the following standard consequence of the Golod–Shafarevich theorem.

Lemma 1: A totally real tower with a prescribed split prime

There exist a rational prime \(p\equiv 1\pmod 4\), a constant \(R_F>0\), and an infinite sequence of totally real number fields

\[ F_1,F_2,F_3,\dots \]

such that, writing \(d_i=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\), we have

\[ d_i\to \infty, \]

the root discriminants are uniformly bounded,

\[ \operatorname{rd}(F_i):=|\operatorname{Disc}(F_i)|^{1/d_i}\le R_F, \]

and \(p\) splits completely in every \(F_i\).

We may take \(p=5\).

Proof

Take \(\ell=3\). Choose a finite set \(S\) of rational primes \(q\neq 3,5\), all satisfying

\[ q\equiv 1\pmod 3, \]

with \(|S|\) sufficiently large.

Let \(G\) be the Galois group of the maximal pro-\(3\) extension of \(\mathbb Q\) that is unramified outside \(S\) and in which the prime \(5\) splits completely. By the Shafarevich presentation theorem for such pro-\(\ell\) Galois groups, \(G\) has a presentation with generator rank \(d(G)\) and relation rank \(r(G)\) satisfying estimates of the form

\[ d(G)\ge |S|-C, \qquad r(G)\le |S|+C, \]

where \(C\) is an absolute constant depending only on the imposed splitting condition at \(5\). For \(|S|\) sufficiently large,

\[ r(G)<\frac{d(G)^2}{4}. \]

The Golod–Shafarevich inequality says that a finite pro-\(\ell\) group must satisfy \(r(G)>d(G)^2/4\). Hence \(G\) is infinite.

Let \(F_i\) be a sequence of finite subextensions of this infinite pro-\(3\) extension with degrees tending to infinity. Since all finite Galois subextensions have odd degree, they are totally real: otherwise complex conjugation would give an element of order \(2\) in a \(3\)-group. The prime \(5\) splits completely by construction.

The extensions are unramified outside the fixed finite set \(S\), and the ramification at primes \(q\in S\) is tame because \(q\neq 3\). For a tame extension, the contribution of \(q\) to the root discriminant is at most \(q\). Therefore every finite subextension satisfies

\[ \operatorname{rd}(F_i)\le \prod_{q\in S}q=:R_F. \]

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

2. Passing to \(K_i=F_i(i)\)

For each \(i\), set

3. An exponential class-number bound in bounded-root-discriminant families

We will need that the class numbers \(h_{K_i}\) grow at most exponentially in \(d_i\).

Lemma 2: Class numbers are exponentially bounded

For every \(R>0\), there exists a constant \(C_R>1\) such that every number field \(K\) of degree \(n\) with

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R \]

satisfies

\[ h_K\le C_R^n. \]

Consequently, for our fields \(K_i\), since \([K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d_i\), there is a constant \(H>1\) such that

\[ h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i} \]

for all \(i\).

Proof

Let \(K\) have degree \(n\), discriminant \(D_K\), and root discriminant at most \(R\). By Minkowski’s ideal-class bound, every ideal class contains an integral ideal \(\mathfrak a\) with

\[ N\mathfrak a \le \left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^{r_2} \frac{n!}{n^n} \sqrt{|D_K|}. \]

Since \(r_2\le n/2\), \(n!/n^n\le 1\), and \(\sqrt{|D_K|}\le R^{n/2}\), there exists a constant \(A_R>1\), depending only on \(R\), such that every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most

\[ A_R^n. \]

Therefore

\[ h_K \le \#\{\text{integral ideals }\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_K:N\mathfrak a\le A_R^n\}. \]

Let \(a_K(m)\) denote the number of integral ideals of norm \(m\). For each rational prime \(\ell\), the Euler factor of \(\zeta_K(s)\) is dominated coefficientwise by

\[ (1-\ell^{-s})^{-n}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ a_K(m)\le d_n(m), \]

where \(d_n(m)\) is the \(n\)-fold divisor function, i.e. the number of ordered \(n\)-tuples \((m_1,\dots,m_n)\in\mathbb N^n\) with

\[ m_1m_2\cdots m_n=m. \]

Hence

\[ h_K \le \sum_{m\le A_R^n}d_n(m) = \#\{(m_1,\dots,m_n)\in\mathbb N^n:m_1\cdots m_n\le A_R^n\}. \]

We now bound this last quantity by \(C_R^n\). Increase \(A_R\), if necessary, so that \(A_R\ge 2\). For each \(m_j\), define

\[ b_j=\left\lceil \log_{A_R} m_j\right\rceil. \]

Then \(m_j\le A_R^{b_j}\), and for \(b_j\ge 1\),

\[ m_j>A_R^{b_j-1}. \]

If \(m_1\cdots m_n\le A_R^n\), then

\[ \sum_{j=1}^n b_j\le 2n. \]

For a fixed vector \((b_1,\dots,b_n)\) with \(\sum b_j=s\), the number of corresponding \(n\)-tuples \((m_1,\dots,m_n)\) is at most \(A_R^s\). Thus

\[ \sum_{m\le A_R^n}d_n(m) \le \sum_{s=0}^{2n} A_R^s \binom{n+s-1}{s}. \]

Since \(s\le 2n\),

\[ \binom{n+s-1}{s}\le \binom{3n}{2n}\le 3^{3n}, \]

and

\[ \sum_{s=0}^{2n}A_R^s\le (2n+1)A_R^{2n}\le (3A_R^2)^n \]

for all sufficiently large \(n\), after increasing the constant to handle small \(n\). Therefore

\[ h_K\le C_R^n \]

for some constant \(C_R>1\) depending only on \(R\).

Applying this to \(K_i\), whose degree is \(2d_i\), gives

\[ h_{K_i}\le C_R^{2d_i}. \]

Set

\[ H=C_R^2. \]

Then

\[ h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i}. \]

\(\square\)

4. Many common-norm translation vectors

The next lemma is the arithmetic core.

Lemma 3: Exponentially many common-relative-norm elements

Let \(F\) be a totally real field of degree \(d\), let

\[ K=F(i), \]

and suppose \(5\) splits completely in \(F\). Let \(h_K\) be the ideal class number of \(K\). Fix an integer \(k\ge 1\), and write

\[ B=5^k. \]

Then there exist a totally positive unit

\[ \eta\in \mathcal O_F^{\times,+} \]

and a set

\[ \mathcal A\subset B^{-1}\mathcal O_K \]

with

\[ |\mathcal A|\ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{2^d h_K} \]

such that every \(\alpha\in\mathcal A\) satisfies

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta. \]

Proof

Since \(5\) splits completely in \(F\), we have

\[ 5\mathcal O_F=\prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5}\mathfrak p, \]

where there are exactly \(d\) primes \(\mathfrak p\), each of norm \(5\).

Since \(5\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the polynomial \(x^2+1\) splits modulo every \(\mathfrak p\mid 5\). Hence each \(\mathfrak p\) splits in \(K=F(i)\):

\[ \mathfrak p\mathcal O_K=\mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\,\overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}. \]

For each \(\mathfrak p\mid 5\), choose one of the two primes above it and call it \(\mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\).

For every vector

\[ \mathbf e=(e_{\mathfrak p})_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \in \{0,1,\dots,k\}^{d}, \]

define the integral ideal

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e} = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}^{\,e_{\mathfrak p}}\, \overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}^{\,k-e_{\mathfrak p}}. \]

There are exactly

\[ (k+1)^d \]

such ideals, and they are all distinct. Moreover,

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\,\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e} = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \left(\mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}\right)^k = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} (\mathfrak p\mathcal O_K)^k = 5^k\mathcal O_K = B\mathcal O_K. \]

Now partition the ideals \(\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\) according to their ideal class in \(\operatorname{Cl}(K)\). Since \(|\operatorname{Cl}(K)|=h_K\), there is a subset \(E\subset \{0,\dots,k\}^d\) with

\[ |E|\ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{h_K} \]

such that all ideals \(\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\), \(\mathbf e\in E\), lie in the same ideal class.

Fix one element \(\mathbf e_0\in E\). For \(\mathbf e\in E\), the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose a generator

\[ \gamma_{\mathbf e}\in K^\times \]

so that

\[ (\gamma_{\mathbf e})=\mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}. \]

Because

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e_0} = B\mathcal O_K, \]

we get

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}\overline{\mathfrak J}_{\mathbf e} = \mathcal O_K. \]

Therefore

\[ (\gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e})=\mathcal O_K. \]

So

\[ u_{\mathbf e}:=\gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e} \]

is a unit of \(K\) fixed by complex conjugation, hence a unit of \(F\). It is totally positive because for every real embedding \(\tau:F\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\),

\[ \tau(u_{\mathbf e})= |\rho(\gamma_{\mathbf e})|^2>0, \]

where \(\rho:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\) is any extension of \(\tau\). Thus

\[ u_{\mathbf e}\in \mathcal O_F^{\times,+}. \]

Also,

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}^{-1} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\,\frac{\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e_0}}{B} \subset B^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Since \(\gamma_{\mathbf e}\in \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}\), we have

\[ \gamma_{\mathbf e}\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Now consider the norm map on units

\[ N_{K/F}:\mathcal O_K^\times\to \mathcal O_F^{\times,+}, \qquad v\mapsto v\bar v. \]

The quotient

\[ \mathcal O_F^{\times,+}/N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times) \]

has size at most \(2^d\). Indeed, for every \(w\in\mathcal O_F^\times\), viewed as a unit of \(K\),

\[ N_{K/F}(w)=w^2. \]

Thus \(N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times)\) contains all squares of units of \(F\), and

\[ \left|\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}/N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times)\right| \le |\mathcal O_F^\times/(\mathcal O_F^\times)^2| \le 2^d. \]

Therefore, by another pigeonhole argument, there is a subset \(E'\subset E\) with

\[ |E'|\ge \frac{|E|}{2^d} \ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{2^d h_K} \]

such that the units \(u_{\mathbf e}\), \(\mathbf e\in E'\), all lie in the same coset of

\[ N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times) \]

inside \(\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}\).

Choose one \(\mathbf e_*\in E'\), and put

\[ \eta:=u_{\mathbf e_*}. \]

For every \(\mathbf e\in E'\), since \(u_{\mathbf e}\) and \(\eta\) are in the same coset, there is a unit \(v_{\mathbf e}\in\mathcal O_K^\times\) such that

\[ v_{\mathbf e}\bar v_{\mathbf e} = \frac{\eta}{u_{\mathbf e}}. \]

Now define

\[ \alpha_{\mathbf e}:=\gamma_{\mathbf e}v_{\mathbf e}. \]

Then

\[ \alpha_{\mathbf e}\bar\alpha_{\mathbf e} = \gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e}\, v_{\mathbf e}\bar v_{\mathbf e} = u_{\mathbf e}\cdot \frac{\eta}{u_{\mathbf e}} = \eta. \]

Also \(\alpha_{\mathbf e}\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_K\), because \(\gamma_{\mathbf e}\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_K\) and \(v_{\mathbf e}\) is a unit.

Finally, the ideals \((\alpha_{\mathbf e})=\mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct as \(\mathbf e\) varies, so the elements \(\alpha_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct. Thus

\[ \mathcal A:=\{\alpha_{\mathbf e}:\mathbf e\in E'\} \]

has the claimed size and satisfies

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta \]

for every \(\alpha\in\mathcal A\). \(\square\)

5. Lattice boxes and unit-distance edges

We now explain how to turn the elements \(\alpha\in\mathcal A\) into unit distances.

Let \(F\) be totally real of degree \(d\), let \(K=F(i)\), and let

\[ \rho_1,\dots,\rho_d:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C \]

be the embeddings extending the real embeddings

\[ \tau_1,\dots,\tau_d:F\hookrightarrow\mathbb R \]

and satisfying \(\rho_j(i)=i\). Thus for \(x\in K\),

\[ \rho_j(\bar x)=\overline{\rho_j(x)}. \]

\[ \tau_j(\eta)>0 \]

for all \(j\), and

\[ \prod_{j=1}^d \tau_j(\eta)=1. \]

\[ B=5^k \]

and set

For \(T>1\), define the Minkowski box

\[ \mathcal B_T(\eta) = \left\{ x\in K: |\rho_j(x)|\le T\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)} \text{ for every }j=1,\dots,d \right\}. \]

We will use \(X_T\) as the abstract set of algebraic points, and then project it to the plane using \(\rho_1\).

Lemma 4: Lattice-point bounds

Assume

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R. \]

There is an absolute constant \(C_1>0\) such that for every \(T>1\),

\[ |X_T|\le \left(C_1B^2T^2\right)^d. \]

Also,

\[ |X_{T-1}| \ge \left( \frac{\pi B^2(T-1)^2}{2R} \right)^d. \]

Proof

We use the Minkowski embedding

\[ K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C^d, \qquad x\mapsto(\rho_1(x),\dots,\rho_d(x)). \]

The covolume of \(\mathcal O_K\) in \(\mathbb C^d\) is

\[ 2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|}. \]

Therefore the covolume of \(L=B^{-1}\mathcal O_K\) is

\[ \operatorname{covol}(L) = B^{-2d}\,2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|}. \]

The volume of the box \(\mathcal B_{T-1}(\eta)\) is

\[ \prod_{j=1}^d \pi\left((T-1)\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}\right)^2 = \pi^d(T-1)^{2d} \prod_{j=1}^d \tau_j(\eta) = \pi^d(T-1)^{2d}, \]

because \(\eta\) is a unit.

We use the following elementary form of van der Corput’s lattice-point lemma: if \(\Lambda\subset\mathbb R^n\) is a lattice and \(C\subset\mathbb R^n\) is centrally symmetric and convex, then

\[ |C\cap\Lambda| \ge \frac{\operatorname{vol}(C)}{2^n\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Here \(n=2d\). Hence

\[ |X_{T-1}| \ge \frac{ \pi^d(T-1)^{2d} }{ 4^d\,B^{-2d}2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|} }. \]

Since

\[ \sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|} = \operatorname{rd}(K)^d \le R^d, \]

we get

\[ |X_{T-1}| \ge \left( \frac{\pi B^2(T-1)^2}{2R} \right)^d. \]

This proves the lower bound.

For the upper bound, we use a packing argument exploiting algebraic integrality.

Let

\[ r_j=T\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}. \]

The \(j\)-th coordinate disk has radius \(r_j\). Cover that disk by \(O((BT)^2)\) subsets of diameter at most

\[ \frac{\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}}{8B}. \]

More explicitly, there is an absolute constant \(C_1\) such that the product box \(\mathcal B_T(\eta)\) can be covered by at most

\[ (C_1B^2T^2)^d \]

product cells, each satisfying the following property: if two points \(x,y\) lie in the same cell, then for every \(j\),

\[ |\rho_j(x-y)| < \frac{\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}}{8B}. \]

We claim that each such cell contains at most one point of \(L\).

Indeed, suppose \(x,y\in L\) are distinct and lie in the same cell. Then

\[ z:=B(x-y)\in \mathcal O_K \]

is a nonzero algebraic integer. Thus

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)|\ge 1. \]

But

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)| = \prod_{j=1}^d |\rho_j(z)|^2. \]

For each \(j\),

\[ |\rho_j(z)| = B|\rho_j(x-y)| < \frac{\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}}{8}. \]

Therefore

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)| < \prod_{j=1}^d \left(\frac{\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}}{8}\right)^2 = 8^{-2d}\prod_{j=1}^d \tau_j(\eta) = 8^{-2d}<1, \]

contradiction.

Thus each cell contains at most one point of \(L\), and therefore

\[ |X_T|\le (C_1B^2T^2)^d. \]

\(\square\)

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le R \]

for all \(i\).

3. Uniform exponential class-number bound

We need a class-number bound depending only exponentially on the degree.

Lemma 2

For every \(R>0\), there exists \(C_R>1\) such that if \(K\) is a number field of degree \(n\) with

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R, \]

then

\[ h_K\le C_R^n. \]

Consequently, since \([K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d_i\), there is a constant \(H>1\) such that

\[ h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i} \]

for all \(i\).

Proof

By Minkowski’s ideal-class bound, every ideal class of \(K\) contains an integral ideal \(\mathfrak a\) with

\[ N\mathfrak a \le \left(\frac4\pi\right)^{r_2} \frac{n!}{n^n} \sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|}. \]

Since \(r_2\le n/2\), \(n!/n^n\le 1\), and

\[ \sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|} \le R^{n/2}, \]

there is a constant \(A=A(R)>1\) such that every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most

\[ A^n. \]

Hence

\[ h_K \le \#\{\mathfrak a\subset\mathcal O_K:N\mathfrak a\le A^n\}. \]

Let \(a_K(m)\) be the number of ideals of norm \(m\). The Euler product for \(\zeta_K(s)\) is coefficientwise dominated by \(\zeta(s)^n\), so

\[ a_K(m)\le d_n(m), \]

where \(d_n(m)\) is the number of ordered factorizations

\[ m=m_1m_2\cdots m_n. \]

Thus

\[ h_K \le \sum_{m\le A^n}d_n(m) = \#\{(m_1,\dots,m_n)\in\mathbb N^n:m_1\cdots m_n\le A^n\}. \]

This last quantity is at most exponential in \(n\). Indeed, after increasing \(A\) if necessary, assume \(A\ge 2\). For each \(m_j\), define

\[ b_j=\lceil \log_A m_j\rceil. \]

If \(m_1\cdots m_n\le A^n\), then

\[ \sum_{j=1}^n b_j\le 2n. \]

For a fixed vector \((b_1,\dots,b_n)\) with sum \(s\), there are at most \(A^s\) choices for \((m_1,\dots,m_n)\). Therefore

\[ \sum_{m\le A^n}d_n(m) \le \sum_{s=0}^{2n}A^s\binom{n+s-1}{s} \le C_R^n \]

for some constant \(C_R>1\). Thus

\[ h_K\le C_R^n. \]

Applying this with \(K=K_i\), \(n=2d_i\), and \(\operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le R\), we get

\[ h_{K_i}\le C_R^{2d_i}. \]

Set

\[ H=C_R^2. \]

Then

\[ h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i}. \]

\(\square\)

4. Many algebraic translations of common relative norm

The next lemma is the arithmetic core.

Lemma 3

Let \(F\) be a totally real field of degree \(d\), let

\[ K=F(i), \]

and suppose \(5\) splits completely in \(F\). Fix \(k\ge 1\), and set

\[ B=5^k. \]

Then there exist a totally positive unit

\[ \eta\in\mathcal O_F^{\times,+} \]

and a set

\[ \mathcal A\subset B^{-1}\mathcal O_K \]

such that

\[ |\mathcal A| \ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{2^d h_K}, \]

and every \(\alpha\in\mathcal A\) satisfies

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta. \]

Proof

Since \(5\) splits completely in \(F\),

\[ 5\mathcal O_F=\prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5}\mathfrak p, \]

with exactly \(d\) primes \(\mathfrak p\), each of norm \(5\).

Each \(\mathfrak p\mid 5\) splits in \(K/F\), so choose one prime \(\mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\) above \(\mathfrak p\), and write

\[ \mathfrak p\mathcal O_K = \mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}. \]

For each vector

\[ \mathbf e=(e_{\mathfrak p})_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \in\{0,1,\dots,k\}^d, \]

define the integral ideal

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e} = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}^{\,e_{\mathfrak p}} \overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}^{\,k-e_{\mathfrak p}}. \]

There are \((k+1)^d\) such ideals, and they are distinct. Moreover,

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e} = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} \left(\mathfrak P_{\mathfrak p}\overline{\mathfrak P}_{\mathfrak p}\right)^k = \prod_{\mathfrak p\mid 5} (\mathfrak p\mathcal O_K)^k = 5^k\mathcal O_K = B\mathcal O_K. \]

Now partition the ideals \(\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\) by their ideal class in \(\operatorname{Cl}(K)\). Some class contains a subset \(E\) of exponent vectors with

\[ |E|\ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{h_K}. \]

Fix \(\mathbf e_0\in E\). For each \(\mathbf e\in E\), the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose \(\gamma_{\mathbf e}\in K^\times\) such that

\[ (\gamma_{\mathbf e})=\mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}. \]

Since

\[ \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e_0} = B\mathcal O_K, \]

we have

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}\overline{\mathfrak J}_{\mathbf e} = \mathcal O_K. \]

Therefore

\[ (\gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e}) = \mathcal O_K. \]

Thus

\[ u_{\mathbf e}:=\gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e} \]

is a unit of \(K\). It is fixed by complex conjugation, hence lies in \(F\). Therefore

\[ u_{\mathbf e}\in\mathcal O_F^\times. \]

For every real embedding \(\tau:F\hookrightarrow\mathbb R\), extending \(\tau\) to \(\rho:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\), we have

\[ \tau(u_{\mathbf e}) = |\rho(\gamma_{\mathbf e})|^2>0. \]

Hence

\[ u_{\mathbf e}\in\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}. \]

Also,

\[ \mathfrak J_{\mathbf e} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak I_{\mathbf e_0}^{-1} = \mathfrak I_{\mathbf e}\frac{\overline{\mathfrak I}_{\mathbf e_0}}{B} \subset B^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Thus \(\gamma_{\mathbf e}\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_K\).

Now consider the relative norm map on units

\[ N_{K/F}:\mathcal O_K^\times\to\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}, \qquad v\mapsto v\bar v. \]

This image contains all squares of units of \(F\), because if \(w\in\mathcal O_F^\times\), then viewed as a unit of \(K\),

\[ N_{K/F}(w)=w^2. \]

Since

\[ \mathcal O_F^\times/(\mathcal O_F^\times)^2 \]

has size \(2^d\), the quotient

\[ \mathcal O_F^{\times,+}/N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times) \]

has size at most \(2^d\).

Therefore, by pigeonhole, there is a subset \(E'\subset E\) with

\[ |E'|\ge \frac{|E|}{2^d} \ge \frac{(k+1)^d}{2^d h_K} \]

such that all \(u_{\mathbf e}\), \(\mathbf e\in E'\), lie in one coset of

\[ N_{K/F}(\mathcal O_K^\times) \]

inside \(\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}\).

Fix \(\mathbf e_*\in E'\), and put

\[ \eta:=u_{\mathbf e_*}. \]

For every \(\mathbf e\in E'\), since \(u_{\mathbf e}\) and \(\eta\) lie in the same coset, there exists \(v_{\mathbf e}\in\mathcal O_K^\times\) such that

\[ v_{\mathbf e}\bar v_{\mathbf e} = \frac{\eta}{u_{\mathbf e}}. \]

Define

\[ \alpha_{\mathbf e}:=\gamma_{\mathbf e}v_{\mathbf e}. \]

Then

\[ \alpha_{\mathbf e}\bar\alpha_{\mathbf e} = \gamma_{\mathbf e}\bar\gamma_{\mathbf e}\, v_{\mathbf e}\bar v_{\mathbf e} = u_{\mathbf e}\frac{\eta}{u_{\mathbf e}} = \eta. \]

Also

\[ \alpha_{\mathbf e}\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

Finally, the fractional ideals \((\alpha_{\mathbf e})=\mathfrak J_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct as \(\mathbf e\) varies, so the elements \(\alpha_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct.

Thus

\[ \mathcal A:=\{\alpha_{\mathbf e}:\mathbf e\in E'\} \]

has the required size, and every \(\alpha\in\mathcal A\) satisfies

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta. \]

\(\square\)

5. Lattice boxes

Let \(F\) be totally real of degree \(d\), \(K=F(i)\), and let

\[ \rho_1,\dots,\rho_d:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb C \]

be the embeddings extending the real embeddings

\[ \tau_1,\dots,\tau_d:F\hookrightarrow\mathbb R \]

with \(\rho_j(i)=i\).

Let \(\eta\in\mathcal O_F^{\times,+}\). Define

\[ a_j=\sqrt{\tau_j(\eta)}>0. \]

Since \(\eta\) is a totally positive unit,

\[ \prod_{j=1}^d a_j^2 = \prod_{j=1}^d \tau_j(\eta) = N_{F/\mathbb Q}(\eta) = 1. \]

Fix \(B\ge 1\), and set

\[ L=B^{-1}\mathcal O_K. \]

For \(T>0\), define

\[ X(T) = \left\{ x\in L: |\rho_j(x)|\le T a_j\ \text{for all }j=1,\dots,d \right\}. \]

Lemma 4

Assume

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R. \]

There is an absolute constant \(C_{\mathrm{box}}>0\) such that for every \(T>1\),

\[ |X(T)|\le \left(C_{\mathrm{box}}B^2T^2\right)^d, \]

and

\[ |X(T-1)| \ge \left( \frac{\pi B^2(T-1)^2}{2R} \right)^d. \]

Proof

Embed \(K\) into \(\mathbb C^d\cong\mathbb R^{2d}\) by

\[ x\mapsto(\rho_1(x),\dots,\rho_d(x)). \]

The covolume of \(\mathcal O_K\) in this embedding is

\[ 2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|}. \]

Therefore the covolume of

\[ L=B^{-1}\mathcal O_K \]

is

\[ \operatorname{covol}(L) = B^{-2d}2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|}. \]

The region defining \(X(T-1)\) is a product of disks of radii \((T-1)a_j\), so its volume is

\[ \prod_{j=1}^d \pi (T-1)^2a_j^2 = \pi^d(T-1)^{2d} \prod_{j=1}^d a_j^2 = \pi^d(T-1)^{2d}. \]

We use the following elementary form of van der Corput’s lattice-point lemma: if \(C\subset\mathbb R^n\) is centrally symmetric and convex and \(\Lambda\) is a lattice, then

\[ |C\cap\Lambda| \ge \frac{\operatorname{vol}(C)}{2^n\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Applying this with \(n=2d\), we get

\[ |X(T-1)| \ge \frac{ \pi^d(T-1)^{2d} }{ 4^d B^{-2d}2^{-d}\sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|} }. \]

Since

\[ \sqrt{|\operatorname{Disc}(K)|} = \operatorname{rd}(K)^d \le R^d, \]

we obtain

\[ |X(T-1)| \ge \left( \frac{\pi B^2(T-1)^2}{2R} \right)^d. \]

This proves the lower bound.

For the upper bound, cover each disk

\[ \{z\in\mathbb C:|z|\le Ta_j\} \]

by at most \(C B^2T^2\) sets of diameter less than

\[ \frac{a_j}{8B}, \]

where \(C\) is an absolute constant. Taking products over \(j=1,\dots,d\), the whole box defining \(X(T)\) is covered by at most

\[ (C_{\mathrm{box}}B^2T^2)^d \]

product cells, with the property that if two points \(x,y\) lie in the same cell, then

\[ |\rho_j(x-y)|<\frac{a_j}{8B} \]

for every \(j\).

We claim each such cell contains at most one point of \(L\). Suppose \(x,y\in L\) are distinct and lie in the same cell. Then

\[ z:=B(x-y)\in\mathcal O_K \]

is a nonzero algebraic integer. Hence

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)|\ge 1. \]

But

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(z)| = \prod_{j=1}^d|\rho_j(z)|^2 < \prod_{j=1}^d\left(\frac{a_j}{8}\right)^2 = 8^{-2d}\prod_{j=1}^d a_j^2 = 8^{-2d}<1, \]

a contradiction. Thus each cell contains at most one point of \(L\), and therefore

\[ |X(T)|\le (C_{\mathrm{box}}B^2T^2)^d. \]

\(\square\)

6. Choosing constants

For each \(i\), apply Lemma 3 to \(F_i\), \(K_i=F_i(i)\), with some fixed integer \(k\ge 1\) to be chosen.

Let

\[ B=5^k. \]

By Lemma 3, there are a totally positive unit

\[ \eta_i\in\mathcal O_{F_i}^{\times,+} \]

and a set

\[ \mathcal A_i\subset B^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i} \]

such that

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta_i \]

for all \(\alpha\in\mathcal A_i\), and

\[ |\mathcal A_i| \ge \frac{(k+1)^{d_i}}{2^{d_i}h_{K_i}}. \]

Since \(h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i}\), we have

\[ |\mathcal A_i| \ge \left(\frac{k+1}{2H}\right)^{d_i}. \]

Define

\[ D:=\frac{k+1}{2H}. \]

Thus

\[ |\mathcal A_i|\ge D^{d_i}. \]

Now define, for \(t>0\),

\[ X_i(t) = \left\{ x\in B^{-1}\mathcal O_{K_i}: |\rho_{i,j}(x)|\le t\sqrt{\tau_{i,j}(\eta_i)} \ \text{for all }j=1,\dots,d_i \right\}. \]

Lemma 4 with \(T=2\) gives

\[ |X_i(1)| \ge \left(\frac{\pi B^2}{2R}\right)^{d_i} \]

and

\[ |X_i(2)| \le \left(4C_{\mathrm{box}}B^2\right)^{d_i}. \]

Set

\[ \lambda:=\frac{\pi B^2}{2R}, \qquad U:=4C_{\mathrm{box}}B^2. \]

Then

\[ |X_i(1)|\ge \lambda^{d_i}, \qquad |X_i(2)|\le U^{d_i}. \]

Choose \(k\) so large that

\[ \lambda>1 \]

and

\[ \Theta:=\frac{D\lambda}{U}>1. \]

This is possible because

\[ \frac{\lambda}{U} = \frac{\pi}{8C_{\mathrm{box}}R} \]

is independent of \(k\), while

\[ D=\frac{k+1}{2H}\to\infty \]

as \(k\to\infty\).

From now on \(k\), hence \(B,D,\lambda,U,\Theta\), are fixed absolute constants.

7. Constructing the point sets

For each \(i\), choose one embedding

\[ \rho_{i,1}:K_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb C \]

extending a real embedding

\[ \tau_{i,1}:F_i\hookrightarrow\mathbb R. \]

Define

\[ a_i:=\sqrt{\tau_{i,1}(\eta_i)}. \]

Now define the finite point set

\[ P_i = \left\{ \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x)}{a_i}:x\in X_i(2) \right\} \subset\mathbb C\simeq\mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\rho_{i,1}\) is an injective field embedding,

\[ |P_i|=|X_i(2)|. \]

Also \(X_i(1)\subset X_i(2)\), so

\[ |P_i| = |X_i(2)| \ge |X_i(1)| \ge \lambda^{d_i}. \]

Since \(\lambda>1\) and \(d_i\to\infty\), we have

\[ |P_i|\to\infty. \]

8. Counting unit distances

Take \(\alpha\in\mathcal A_i\). Since

\[ \alpha\bar\alpha=\eta_i, \]

we have, for every \(j\),

\[ |\rho_{i,j}(\alpha)|^2 = \tau_{i,j}(\eta_i), \]

so

\[ |\rho_{i,j}(\alpha)| = \sqrt{\tau_{i,j}(\eta_i)}. \]

Thus, if \(x\in X_i(1)\), then for every \(j\),

\[ |\rho_{i,j}(x+\alpha)| \le |\rho_{i,j}(x)|+|\rho_{i,j}(\alpha)| \le \sqrt{\tau_{i,j}(\eta_i)} + \sqrt{\tau_{i,j}(\eta_i)} = 2\sqrt{\tau_{i,j}(\eta_i)}. \]

Hence

\[ x+\alpha\in X_i(2). \]

Therefore for every pair \((x,\alpha)\in X_i(1)\times\mathcal A_i\), the two points

\[ \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x)}{a_i}, \qquad \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x+\alpha)}{a_i} \]

belong to \(P_i\), and their Euclidean distance is

\[ \left| \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x+\alpha)-\rho_{i,1}(x)}{a_i} \right| = \frac{|\rho_{i,1}(\alpha)|}{a_i} = 1. \]

Moreover, the map

\[ (x,\alpha) \mapsto \left( \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x)}{a_i}, \frac{\rho_{i,1}(x+\alpha)}{a_i} \right) \]

is injective. Indeed, if two pairs \((x,\alpha)\) and \((y,\beta)\) produce the same ordered pair of points, then injectivity of \(\rho_{i,1}\) gives \(x=y\), and then \(x+\alpha=y+\beta\), so \(\alpha=\beta\).

Thus the number of directed unit-distance pairs in \(P_i\) is at least

\[ |\mathcal A_i|\,|X_i(1)|. \]

Since \(u(P_i)\) counts undirected unit-distance pairs, equivalently half the number of directed unit-distance pairs, we get

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12|\mathcal A_i|\,|X_i(1)|. \]

Using the bounds above,

\[ u(P_i) \ge \frac12 D^{d_i}\lambda^{d_i} = \frac12(D\lambda)^{d_i} = \frac12(\Theta U)^{d_i}. \]

9. Extracting a power saving

We know

\[ |P_i|=|X_i(2)|\le U^{d_i}. \]

Let

\[ \delta:=\frac{\log\Theta}{2\log U}. \]

Since \(\Theta>1\), we have \(\delta>0\).

For all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ \frac12\Theta^{d_i} \ge \Theta^{d_i/2}. \]

Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ u(P_i) \ge U^{d_i}\Theta^{d_i/2} = U^{d_i(1+\delta)}. \]

Since

\[ |P_i|\le U^{d_i}, \]

we obtain

\[ u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

Discarding finitely many initial terms and relabeling, we get an infinite sequence of finite point sets \(P_i\subset\mathbb R^2\) such that

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for a fixed absolute constant \(\delta>0\).

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)
