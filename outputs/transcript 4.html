System prompt

The provided theorem was recently proved, and the result is now accepted by the mathematical community. Your task is to independently construct a proof.

Round 1

User

Theorem. There exist an absolute constant $\delta > 0$ and an infinite sequence $\{P_i\}$ of finite point sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $|P_i| \to \infty$ and $u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}$ for all $i$, where $u(P_i) = \frac{1}{2} | \{ (p,q) \in P_i \times P_i : \|p-q\|_2 = 1 \} |$ is the number of unit-distance pairs.

Remark. This result disproves the Erdős Unit Distance Conjecture.

As a first step, brainstorm and propose three most promising approaches to prove this theorem. Explore each approach to sufficient depth, and then rank them from most to least promising.

Assistant

Below is how I would organize the search for a proof. The common theme is that a superlinear number of unit distances is easiest to get if the point set has many “unit almost-periods.”

For a finite set \(P\subset \mathbb R^2\) and a unit vector \(v\in S^1\), write

\[ M_v(P):=\bigl|\{p\in P:p+v\in P\}\bigr|=|P\cap(P-v)|. \]

Then

\[ u(P)\ge \frac12\sum_{v\in V} M_v(P) \]

for every finite \(V\subset S^1\). Thus a very clean sufficient mechanism is:

Find \(P\) of size \(N\) and \(D\) distinct unit vectors \(v\) such that

\[ |P\cap(P-v)|\ge cN \]

for each of them. Then

\[ u(P)\ge c' DN. \]

If \(D\ge N^\delta\), we are done.

So the main task is to construct finite planar sets with polynomially many unit translations under which the set has large overlap.

A quick warning: the most naive version of this idea fails. If \(V\subset S^1\), then the map

\[ \{v,w\}\mapsto v+w \]

is essentially injective, because the sum of two points on a circle determines the chord. Hence \(|V+V|\asymp |V|^2\). Therefore the simple two-layer construction \(P=V\cup(V+V)\) gives only linearly many unit distances. Any successful proof has to compress many unit translations more efficiently than this.

With that in mind, here are the three approaches I would pursue.

1. Cut-and-project / quasicrystal construction with many unit translation vectors

Core idea

Use a high-dimensional lattice \(\Lambda\subset \mathbb R^2_{\mathrm{phys}}\times \mathbb R^m_{\mathrm{int}}\), project it to the physical plane, and select only those lattice points whose internal projection lies in a window. This is the standard cut-and-project/model-set mechanism.

Let

\[ \pi:\mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^m\to \mathbb R^2 \]

be the physical projection and

\[ \pi^\star:\mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^m\to \mathbb R^m \]

the internal projection. Given a physical window \(\Omega_R\subset \mathbb R^2\), say a disk or square of diameter \(R\), and an internal window \(W\subset \mathbb R^m\), define

\[ P=P(\Lambda,\Omega_R,W) :=\{\pi(\lambda):\lambda\in \Lambda,\ \pi(\lambda)\in \Omega_R,\ \pi^\star(\lambda)\in W\}. \]

Assume \(\pi\) is injective on \(\Lambda\), so \(P\) is genuinely a set.

The size is approximately

\[ |P|\sim \frac{\operatorname{area}(\Omega_R)\operatorname{vol}(W)}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Now suppose \(\tau\in \Lambda\) satisfies

\[ \|\pi(\tau)\|_2=1. \]

Then translation by the unit vector \(v=\pi(\tau)\) sends \(P\) to itself on the portion where both the physical and internal windows overlap. More precisely,

\[ |\{p\in P:p+v\in P\}| \]

is approximately

\[ \frac{\operatorname{area}(\Omega_R\cap(\Omega_R-v))\operatorname{vol}(W\cap(W-\pi^\star(\tau)))}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

If \(R\) is large and if

\[ \operatorname{vol}(W\cap(W-\pi^\star(\tau)))\ge \eta \operatorname{vol}(W) \]

for some fixed \(\eta>0\), then this unit vector contributes \(\gg |P|\) unit-distance pairs.

Thus the strategy becomes:

Construct a cut-and-project scheme with many lattice vectors \(\tau_1,\dots,\tau_D\in\Lambda\) such that

\[ \|\pi(\tau_j)\|_2=1 \]

and the internal shifts \(\pi^\star(\tau_j)\) all move \(W\) by only a bounded-overlap loss.

Then

\[ u(P)\gg D|P|. \]

The issue is to make \(D\) polynomially large in \(|P|\).

A useful sufficient lemma

A clean target lemma would be the following.

Polynomial-cost model-set lemma

There exist constants \(C,\eta>0\) and arbitrarily large integers \(D\) for which one can find:

  1. a cut-and-project lattice \(\Lambda_D\subset \mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^m\),
  2. an internal window \(W_D\),
  3. \(D\) lattice vectors \(\tau_1,\dots,\tau_D\in\Lambda_D\),

such that:

  • \(\|\pi(\tau_j)\|_2=1\) for all \(j\);
  • the physical unit vectors \(\pi(\tau_j)\) are distinct;
  • the window overlaps satisfy
\[ \operatorname{vol}(W_D\cap(W_D-\pi^\star(\tau_j)))\ge \eta \operatorname{vol}(W_D); \]
  • the model-set density
\[ \rho_D:=\frac{\operatorname{vol}(W_D)}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda_D)} \]

has only polynomial cost:

\[ \rho_D\le D^C. \]

Then choose \(\Omega_R\) with area roughly \(D^{C+2}/\rho_D\). This gives

\[ |P_D|\asymp D^{C+2} \]

and

\[ u(P_D)\gg D|P_D|. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_D)\gg |P_D|^{1+1/(C+2)}. \]

So this lemma would prove the theorem with

\[ \delta=\frac{1}{C+2}. \]

Why this is promising

This approach isolates the problem into a geometric/arithmetic compression question:

Can one realize \(D\) distinct unit translations at only polynomial density cost?

The naive construction with \(D\) arbitrary unit vectors has internal dimension about \(D\), and the required window volume usually grows exponentially in \(D\). That gives only \(D\sim \log N\), hence no polynomial improvement. The winning version must be much more efficient: many unit directions must live in a low-complexity cut-and-project scheme.

There are several plausible sources for such efficient schemes:

1. Low-dimensional irrational cylinder lattices

One can try to build lattices \(\Lambda\subset \mathbb R^2\times \mathbb R^m\) for fixed small \(m\) such that the cylinder

\[ S^1\times B_H^{m} \]

contains polynomially many lattice points. Each such lattice point gives a unit physical vector. If

\[ \#\bigl(\Lambda\cap(S^1\times B_H^m)\bigr)\gg H^\kappa, \]

then, with \(W\) a slightly larger internal ball, we obtain \(D\gg H^\kappa\) unit translations at polynomial window cost.

This reduces the problem to a lattice-point count on a degenerate quadratic surface.

2. Arithmetic lattices from number fields

Another source is a number-field lattice. Suppose \(K\) has one distinguished complex embedding \(\sigma_0:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\), used as the physical plane, and the other embeddings form the internal space. One searches for many algebraic numbers \(\alpha\) in a fixed module such that

\[ |\sigma_0(\alpha)|=1 \]

while the other conjugates remain bounded by a polynomial parameter. These \(\alpha\)’s become unit translation vectors in the physical plane.

The difficulty is to avoid only getting logarithmically many such elements from unit groups. One needs a genuinely higher-dimensional family of elements with one conjugate on the unit circle, not merely powers of a fixed unit.

3. Compressed cyclotomic or toric modules

Cyclotomic modules contain many unit physical vectors, namely roots of unity, but the full cyclotomic rank is usually too large. A more refined construction could try to compress many torsion-like directions into a smaller internal-cost model set. This is attractive because roots of unity automatically give exact unit vectors; the challenge is polynomial density cost.

Strengths

  • It directly produces many unit translations.
  • Edge counting is robust and transparent.
  • Boundary effects are easy to control.
  • It naturally explains how to get \(u(P)\sim D|P|\).
  • It can be made compatible with the known \(O(N^{4/3})\) incidence upper bounds by choosing \(D\le N^{1/3}\).

Main obstacle

The whole problem is pushed into proving the polynomial-cost model-set lemma. Naive high-rank model sets cost exponentially much density, yielding only \(N^{1+o(1)}\). The key breakthrough would be finding a dimension-efficient lattice/window pair with polynomially many unit physical lattice vectors.

2. Layered / Cartesian-product incidence construction

Core idea

It is enough to build a bipartite unit-distance configuration. If \(C,Q\subset \mathbb R^2\) and many pairs \((c,q)\in C\times Q\) satisfy

\[ \|c-q\|_2=1, \]

then for

\[ P=C\cup Q \]

we get many unit distances. Thus we can search for many incidences between points and congruent unit circles.

A convenient special case is a Cartesian product

\[ P=A\times B. \]

Then the number of directed unit-distance pairs is

\[ \sum_{\substack{x\in A-A,\ y\in B-B\\ x^2+y^2=1}} r_{A-A}(x)\,r_{B-B}(y), \]

where

\[ r_{A-A}(x)=|\{(a,a')\in A^2:a-a'=x\}|. \]

Thus we want many unit vectors \((x,y)\in S^1\) for which \(x\) is a popular difference of \(A\) and \(y\) is a popular difference of \(B\).

The ordinary integer-grid construction takes \(A=B=\{1,\dots,m\}\), and then unit vectors correspond to many representations of one integer as a sum of two squares. That gives only subpolynomial improvement. To prove the theorem by this route, one needs a stronger construction of popular circle differences.

More flexible layered version

A more adaptable construction uses horizontal layers.

Let

\[ P=\bigcup_{j=1}^t P_j, \qquad P_j=(A+s_j)\times \{y_j\}, \]

where \(A\subset \mathbb R\) has size \(L\). Then

\[ |P|=tL. \]

Consider two layers \(i,j\). A point \((a+s_i,y_i)\in P_i\) and a point \((a'+s_j,y_j)\in P_j\) are at unit distance if

\[ (a-a'+s_i-s_j)^2+(y_i-y_j)^2=1. \]

Equivalently, for some difference \(d=a-a'\in A-A\),

\[ (d+s_i-s_j)^2+(y_i-y_j)^2=1. \]

If this happens for a popular difference \(d\), then the pair of layers contributes about \(r_{A-A}(d)\) unit distances.

For example, if \(A=\{0,1,\dots,L-1\}\), then differences \(d\) with \(|d|\le L/2\) have multiplicity \(\gg L\). Thus each compatible pair of layers contributes \(\gg L\) unit distances.

So if we can find \(t\) layers with

\[ E_{\mathrm{comp}}\gg t^{1+\varepsilon} \]

compatible layer pairs, then

\[ u(P)\gg L\,t^{1+\varepsilon}. \]

Since \(N=|P|=tL\), this gives

\[ u(P)\gg N t^\varepsilon. \]

Choosing \(t=N^\alpha\) gives

\[ u(P)\gg N^{1+\alpha\varepsilon}. \]

Thus it suffices to construct a superlinear number of compatible layer pairs.

Reformulation as a real incidence problem

The compatibility condition is

\[ (d+s_i-s_j)^2+(y_i-y_j)^2=1 \]

for some popular horizontal difference \(d\in A-A\). Equivalently,

\[ (s_i,y_i)-(s_j,y_j)\in S^1-(d,0). \]

So we are looking for many pairs among anchor points

\[ z_j=(s_j,y_j) \]

whose difference lies in a union of horizontally shifted unit circles.

This suggests using known incidence extremizer heuristics. The usual Szemerédi–Trotter lower-bound constructions use grids and line families. Here we need a version for congruent circles, or for translates of a fixed algebraic curve.

Possible routes:

1. Rational parametrization of the unit circle

Use

\[ \phi(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right). \]

We want many values of \(t\) for which both coordinates of \(\phi(t)\) land in popular difference sets. This becomes a question about constructing sets \(A,B\subset \mathbb R\) such that the rational functions

\[ \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad \frac{2t}{1+t^2} \]

simultaneously take many values in \(A-A\) and \(B-B\) with large multiplicity.

This might be attacked through algebraic number theory, Pell-type parametrizations, or elliptic-curve/fiber-product constructions.

2. Reverse-engineering a congruent-circle incidence extremizer

We can try to imitate point-line incidence extremizers using unit circles. A unit circle with center \((a,b)\) has local graph

\[ y=b+\sqrt{1-(x-a)^2}. \]

Near a common tangent point these curves look like translated parabolas. One could attempt to build an exact algebraic version of the usual grid-line construction, replacing lines by congruent-circle arcs and using a parametrization to avoid approximation.

3. Algebraic difference sets

The Cartesian formula suggests the following target lemma.

Find finite \(A,B\subset\mathbb R\) and a set \(T\subset S^1\) with \(|T|\ge |A|^\kappa\), such that for every \((x,y)\in T\), both \(x\) and \(y\) are popular differences:

\[ r_{A-A}(x)\gg |A|^c,\qquad r_{B-B}(y)\gg |B|^c. \]

Then \(A\times B\) gives many unit distances.

Strengths

  • Very concrete and low-dimensional.
  • Edge counting is exact.
  • It reduces the theorem to additive-combinatorial structure of difference sets.
  • It avoids building a full approximate additive group invariant under many unit translations.

Main obstacle

The ordinary grid/Pythagorean method gives only \(N^{1+o(1)}\). To get \(N^{1+\delta}\), one needs polynomially many popular circle differences. That requires a genuinely new construction of one-dimensional sets \(A,B\) or of layered anchors \((s_j,y_j)\). The unit circle has strong convexity constraints, so simple small-sumset ideas fail.

3. Finite-field construction followed by algebraic lifting to \(\mathbb R^2\)

Core idea

Over a finite field \(\mathbb F_q\), the unit-distance graph is extremely dense. Let

\[ S_q=\{(x,y)\in \mathbb F_q^2:x^2+y^2=1\}. \]

For odd \(q\), \(|S_q|=q+O(1)\). Therefore the graph on \(\mathbb F_q^2\) connecting \(p\) to \(p+v\) for \(v\in S_q\) has approximately

\[ q^2\cdot q=q^3 \]

directed edges among \(q^2\) vertices.

This is the ideal translation-invariant model: every point has about \(q\) unit neighbors. If one could transfer even a power-saving portion of this structure to the real plane, the theorem would follow.

Of course the full finite-field graph cannot be realized in \(\mathbb R^2\) with the same parameters, because that would exceed the known \(O(N^{4/3})\) incidence barrier. So one would thin the finite-field configuration or allow some controlled blow-up, aiming for final exponent \(1+\delta\le 4/3\).

Possible lifting scheme

Choose a subgraph \(G_q\) of the finite-field unit-distance graph with

\[ |V(G_q)|\asymp q^B,\qquad |E(G_q)|\asymp q^{B+\kappa} \]

for some \(\kappa>0\), but sparse enough that a real realization is not ruled out by incidence bounds.

Then consider the algebraic realization variety

\[ X_{G_q} = \left\{(p_v)_{v\in V(G_q)}\in (\mathbb A^2)^{V(G_q)}: \|p_u-p_v\|^2=1\ \text{for every }uv\in E(G_q) \right\}. \]

The finite-field construction gives an \(\mathbb F_q\)-point of this variety. The challenge is to prove that a related variety has a real point with distinct vertices.

Possible routes:

1. Choose \(G_q\) with a rational parametrization

Instead of taking an arbitrary subgraph, choose one whose realization space is explicitly parameterized. For example, a layered graph where vertices are formal sums of unit directions may have equations that reduce to a small set of algebraic identities.

Then the finite-field construction supplies the combinatorial blueprint, while the parametrization is used to build an actual real realization.

2. Hensel lifting and weak approximation

One can try to lift the finite-field realization to a \(p\)-adic realization and then use an arithmetic family with real points. The ideal situation would be:

  • a scheme \(\mathcal X\) over \(\mathbb Z\);
  • many finite-field points on special fibers;
  • a geometrically irreducible component with a smooth point;
  • weak approximation or real patchworking producing a real point.

This is delicate, because finite-field solubility alone does not imply real solubility.

3. Modular-to-rational circle parametrization

Use the rational parametrization

\[ \phi(t)=\left(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\frac{2t}{1+t^2}\right) \]

and select \(t\)-values with good additive structure modulo \(q\). Then lift them to rationals or algebraic numbers while preserving enough exact identities.

This would connect the finite-field construction to the layered/Cartesian-product approach above.

Parameter accounting

Suppose the finite-field blueprint gives \(q^{B+\kappa}\) edges and the lifting procedure produces a real point set of size at most \(q^{B'}\), retaining at least \(q^{A}\) unit edges. We need

\[ A>B'. \]

Then

\[ u(P)\ge |P|^{A/B'}. \]

Thus

\[ \delta=\frac{A}{B'}-1. \]

Even a small positive gap \(A-B'\) suffices.

Strengths

  • Finite fields already provide the desired translation-invariant dense unit graph.
  • The method could explain the construction conceptually: real examples as lifted shadows of finite-field ones.
  • Algebraic geometry gives powerful tools for transferring structured polynomial configurations.

Main obstacle

Finite-field realizability does not automatically imply real realizability. Also, modular coincidences often disappear when lifted to characteristic \(0\). One needs a carefully chosen subconfiguration whose incidence equations are algebraically dependent in a way that survives over \(\mathbb R\).

Ranking

Rank 1: Cut-and-project / quasicrystal method

This is the most promising in my view.

It gives the cleanest route to the theorem:

  1. construct many unit lattice translations;
  2. choose a model set window with large overlaps;
  3. count edges by translation invariance.

The proof would reduce to a precise polynomial-cost model-set lemma. This is exactly the kind of structure one expects in a successful construction: many exact unit directions, many points stable under those directions, and controllable boundary loss.

The main technical challenge is nontrivial but well isolated: build a dimension-efficient cut-and-project scheme with polynomially many unit physical vectors.

Rank 2: Layered / Cartesian-product incidence construction

This is second-most promising because it is concrete and entirely real. It reduces the problem to explicit additive-combinatorial statements about difference sets and the unit circle.

The layered version is especially attractive: if one can find \(t\) horizontal layers with \(t^{1+\varepsilon}\) compatible pairs, then one immediately gets

\[ u(P)\gg |P|^{1+\delta}. \]

The challenge is to construct polynomially many compatible layer pairs without collapsing back to the ordinary grid/Pythagorean mechanism, which only gives \(N^{1+o(1)}\).

Rank 3: Finite-field lifting

This is conceptually powerful but technically the riskiest.

Finite fields contain the perfect model: \(q^2\) points and about \(q^3\) unit-distance pairs. However, transferring such configurations to \(\mathbb R^2\) while preserving exact unit distances and distinctness is difficult. A successful proof would likely need to combine finite-field blueprints with either:

  • a rational parametrization,
  • a model-set compression mechanism,
  • or an algebraic lifting/patchworking argument.

So I would treat this as a valuable source of designs and intuition, but not as the first route to push unless one has a very structured finite-field subgraph whose realization variety can be explicitly controlled.

Summary table

| Rank | Approach | Main object to build | Why promising | Main obstruction | |---:|---|---|---|---| | 1 | Cut-and-project / quasicrystals | Model set with \(D\) unit translations and polynomial density cost | Directly gives \(u(P)\gg D|P|\) | Need efficient lattice/window with many unit physical vectors | | 2 | Layered / Cartesian incidence | Many compatible layer pairs or popular circle differences | Concrete real-variable construction | Need polynomially many popular differences on the unit circle | | 3 | Finite-field lifting | Lift a dense finite-field unit-distance graph to \(\mathbb R^2\) | Finite fields have the ideal dense model | Real lifting and exact preservation are delicate |

If I were beginning a proof, I would first try to prove the polynomial-cost model-set lemma from Approach 1. The other two approaches can be viewed as alternate ways of producing the same underlying phenomenon: many unit directions acting on a finite planar set with large overlaps.

Assistant

We will implement the cut-and-project strategy using CM fields.

The key idea is this:

  • A CM field \(K\) has a conjugation \(x\mapsto \overline{x}\) such that, for every complex embedding \(\sigma:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\),
\[ \sigma(\overline{x})=\overline{\sigma(x)}. \]

So if

\[ \theta=\frac{z}{\overline z}, \]

then

\[ \theta \overline\theta=1, \]

and therefore

\[ |\sigma(\theta)|=1 \]

for every complex embedding \(\sigma\). In particular, under one chosen embedding \(\sigma_0\), \(\sigma_0(\theta)\) is an exact Euclidean unit vector in \(\mathbb C\simeq \mathbb R^2\).

Thus norm-one elements of a CM field are perfect unit-translation vectors. The proof constructs exponentially many such translations inside a fractional ideal of only exponentially bounded covolume, and then uses a model-set window in the full Minkowski embedding.

The proof has two arithmetic inputs, both standard consequences of class-field-tower theory and analytic class-number estimates.

1. Arithmetic input

We use the following two standard facts.

Lemma 1: Split CM towers of bounded root discriminant

There exist:

  • a rational prime \(\ell\),
  • a constant \(R_0<\infty\),
  • an infinite sequence of CM fields \(K_i\),

such that, writing

\[ [K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d_i, \]

we have:

  1. \(d_i\to\infty\);

  2. the root discriminants are uniformly bounded:

    \[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i):=|\Delta_{K_i}|^{1/[K_i:\mathbb Q]}\le R_0; \]
  3. the prime \(\ell\) splits completely in every \(K_i\).

This is a standard \(T\)-split Golod–Shafarevich/class-field-tower construction. One starts with a CM field \(K_0\) and a finite set \(T\) of primes above a rational prime \(\ell\), arranged so that the maximal unramified pro-\(p\) extension in which all primes of \(T\) split completely is infinite. Passing to conjugation-stable finite layers gives CM fields. Since the tower is unramified over \(K_0\), the root discriminant remains constant, and since the primes above \(\ell\) split at every level, \(\ell\) splits completely throughout the tower.

Lemma 2: Exponential class-number bound in bounded root-discriminant families

For every \(R_0\) there is a constant \(H=H(R_0)\) such that every CM field \(K\) of degree \(2d\) with

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R_0 \]

satisfies

\[ h_K\le H^d, \]

where \(h_K\) is the class number of \(K\).

This follows from the analytic class-number formula together with standard residue bounds for \(\zeta_K(s)\) and regulator lower bounds. Concretely, for fixed root-discriminant bound, one has

\[ \operatorname{Res}_{s=1}\zeta_K(s)\le C_1(R_0)^d, \]

and Friedman's regulator lower bound gives

\[ R_K\ge C_2^{-d} \]

up to harmless root-of-unity factors, which are also at most exponential in \(d\). The analytic class-number formula then gives

\[ h_K\le H(R_0)^d. \]

From now on fix \(\ell,R_0,H\) as above.

2. Parameter choice

We will choose a positive integer \(m\) once and for all.

For later use define

\[ C_m:=(1+2\ell^m)^2. \]

Also define the edge-growth base

\[ L_m:=\frac{(m+1)\pi \ell^{2m}}{2HR_0}. \]

We claim we can choose \(m\) so that

\[ L_m>C_m. \]

Indeed,

\[ \frac{L_m}{C_m} = \frac{(m+1)\pi \ell^{2m}}{2HR_0(1+2\ell^m)^2}. \]

As \(m\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{\ell^{2m}}{(1+2\ell^m)^2}\to \frac14, \]

so

\[ \frac{L_m}{C_m} \sim \frac{\pi(m+1)}{8HR_0}\to\infty. \]

Thus choose \(m\) large enough that

\[ A:=\frac{L_m}{C_m}>1. \]

Now choose

\[ 0<\delta<\frac{\log A}{\log C_m}. \]

Equivalently,

\[ L_m>C_m^{1+\delta}. \]

This \(\delta>0\) is absolute.

3. Work inside one CM field

Fix one of the CM fields \(K=K_i\), and write

\[ [K:\mathbb Q]=2d. \]

Let \(F\subset K\) be the maximal totally real subfield, so \([F:\mathbb Q]=d\).

Choose one embedding from each complex-conjugate pair:

\[ \sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_d:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C. \]

We identify

\[ \mathbb C^d\simeq \mathbb R^{2d}. \]

The physical plane will be the first coordinate \(\sigma_1\). The remaining coordinates are internal.

Define the Minkowski embedding

\[ \sigma:K\longrightarrow \mathbb C^d, \qquad \sigma(\alpha)=(\sigma_1(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_d(\alpha)). \]

Because \(\ell\) splits completely in \(K\), we may factor

\[ \ell\mathcal O_K = \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak q_j\overline{\mathfrak q_j}, \]

where each \(\mathfrak q_j\) has norm \(\ell\), and conjugation pairs \(\mathfrak q_j\) with \(\overline{\mathfrak q_j}\).

4. Exponentially many norm-one translations

Consider the exponent cube

\[ \mathcal E:=\{0,1,\dots,m\}^d. \]

For \(\mathbf e=(e_1,\dots,e_d)\in\mathcal E\), define the ideal

\[ \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e} := \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak q_j^{e_j}. \]

The map

\[ \mathbf e\mapsto [\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}]\in \operatorname{Cl}(K) \]

takes \((m+1)^d\) values into the class group of size \(h_K\le H^d\). Therefore some fiber \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal E\) has size at least

\[ |\mathcal F| \ge \frac{(m+1)^d}{h_K} \ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d. \]

Fix one element \(\mathbf e_*\in\mathcal F\).

For every \(\mathbf e\in\mathcal F\), the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e_*}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose a generator \(z_{\mathbf e}\in K^\times\), so

\[ (z_{\mathbf e}) = \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e_*}^{-1}. \]

Now define

\[ \theta_{\mathbf e} := \frac{z_{\mathbf e}}{\overline{z_{\mathbf e}}}. \]

Then

\[ \theta_{\mathbf e}\overline{\theta_{\mathbf e}}=1. \]

Hence, for every complex embedding \(\sigma_r\),

\[ |\sigma_r(\theta_{\mathbf e})|=1. \]

In particular,

\[ |\sigma_1(\theta_{\mathbf e})|=1. \]

Thus each \(\sigma_1(\theta_{\mathbf e})\) is a unit vector in the physical plane.

Moreover, the \(\theta_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct. Indeed, the valuation of \((\theta_{\mathbf e})\) at \(\mathfrak q_j\) is

\[ e_j-e_{*,j}. \]

So equality of ideals \((\theta_{\mathbf e})=(\theta_{\mathbf f})\) forces \(e_j=f_j\) for every \(j\), hence \(\mathbf e=\mathbf f\). Thus

\[ \Theta:=\{\theta_{\mathbf e}:\mathbf e\in\mathcal F\} \]

has size

\[ |\Theta|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d. \]

Finally, each \(\theta_{\mathbf e}\) lies in the fractional ideal

\[ \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K. \]

Indeed, the valuations of \(\theta_{\mathbf e}\) at primes above \(\ell\) lie between \(-m\) and \(m\), and there are no other prime factors. Therefore multiplying by \(\ell^m\) clears all denominators.

So we have constructed at least

\[ \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d \]

distinct unit physical translation vectors lying in the additive lattice

\[ \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K. \]

5. The model set

Let

\[ \Lambda:=\sigma(\ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K)\subset \mathbb C^d. \]

This is a full-rank lattice in \(\mathbb R^{2d}\).

Let

\[ B_d:=\left\{z\in \mathbb C^d:\|z\|_2<\sqrt d\right\} \]

be the Euclidean ball of radius \(\sqrt d\).

For \(t\in \mathbb C^d\), define

\[ X_t := \left\{ \alpha\in \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K: \sigma(\alpha)\in t+B_d \right\}. \]

Then define the planar point set

\[ P_t := \{\sigma_1(\alpha):\alpha\in X_t\} \subset \mathbb C\simeq \mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\sigma_1:K\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\) is injective,

\[ |P_t|=|X_t|. \]

6. Uniform upper bound for \(|P_t|\)

We need a uniform exponential upper bound for \(|P_t|\).

Let \(\alpha,\beta\in \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K\), \(\alpha\ne\beta\). Then

\[ \gamma:=\alpha-\beta\in \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K\setminus\{0\}. \]

Thus

\[ \ell^m\gamma\in\mathcal O_K\setminus\{0\}. \]

Therefore

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\ell^m\gamma)|\ge 1. \]

Since \([K:\mathbb Q]=2d\),

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\ell^m)| = \ell^{2md}. \]

Hence

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\gamma)| \ge \ell^{-2md}. \]

But

\[ |N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\gamma)| = \prod_{r=1}^d |\sigma_r(\gamma)|^2. \]

By AM-GM,

\[ \sum_{r=1}^d |\sigma_r(\gamma)|^2 \ge d\left(\prod_{r=1}^d |\sigma_r(\gamma)|^2\right)^{1/d} \ge d\ell^{-2m}. \]

Thus

\[ \|\sigma(\alpha)-\sigma(\beta)\|_2 = \|\sigma(\gamma)\|_2 \ge \sqrt d\,\ell^{-m}. \]

So the lattice \(\Lambda\) has minimum distance at least \(\sqrt d\,\ell^{-m}\).

Now all points of \(\Lambda\cap(t+B_d)\) lie in a ball of radius \(\sqrt d\). Packing balls of radius \(\frac12\sqrt d\,\ell^{-m}\) around them gives

\[ |X_t| \le \left( 1+2\ell^m \right)^{2d}. \]

Therefore

\[ |P_t| \le C_m^d, \]

where

\[ C_m=(1+2\ell^m)^2. \]

7. Averaging the number of unit-translation incidences

For \(\theta\in\Theta\), define

\[ M_\theta(t) := \left| \left\{ \alpha\in X_t: \alpha+\theta\in X_t \right\} \right|. \]

Each such \(\alpha\) gives a unit-distance pair in \(P_t\), because

\[ \left| \sigma_1(\alpha+\theta)-\sigma_1(\alpha) \right| = |\sigma_1(\theta)| = 1. \]

Let

\[ I(t):=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta} M_\theta(t). \]

We will show that some translate \(t\) satisfies

\[ I(t)\ge L_m^d. \]

7.1 Covolume computation

The covolume of \(\sigma(\mathcal O_K)\) in \(\mathbb C^d\) is

\[ 2^{-d}\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}. \]

Since

\[ \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K \]

has absolute ideal norm

\[ \ell^{-2md}, \]

we get

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Lambda) = 2^{-d}\sqrt{|\Delta_K|}\,\ell^{-2md}. \]

Because

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K)\le R_0 \]

and \([K:\mathbb Q]=2d\),

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_K|} \le R_0^d. \]

Therefore

\[ \operatorname{covol}(\Lambda) \le 2^{-d}R_0^d\ell^{-2md}. \]

7.2 Average number of points in the window

The volume of \(B_d\subset\mathbb R^{2d}\) is

\[ \operatorname{vol}(B_d) = \frac{\pi^d d^d}{d!}. \]

Since \(d!\le d^d\),

\[ \operatorname{vol}(B_d)\ge \pi^d. \]

Hence

\[ \frac{\operatorname{vol}(B_d)}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)} \ge \frac{\pi^d}{2^{-d}R_0^d\ell^{-2md}} = \left(\frac{2\pi \ell^{2m}}{R_0}\right)^d. \]

7.3 Average overlap for each unit translation

For every \(\theta\in\Theta\), we have

\[ \|\sigma(\theta)\|_2^2 = \sum_{r=1}^d |\sigma_r(\theta)|^2 = d. \]

Thus

\[ \|\sigma(\theta)\|_2=\sqrt d. \]

The intersection

\[ B_d\cap(B_d-\sigma(\theta)) \]

contains a ball of radius \(\sqrt d/2\). Therefore

\[ \operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_d\cap(B_d-\sigma(\theta))\bigr) \ge 4^{-d}\operatorname{vol}(B_d). \]

Now average over \(t\) modulo the lattice \(\Lambda\). For any measurable bounded set \(S\subset \mathbb C^d\),

\[ \mathbb E_t|\Lambda\cap(t+S)| = \frac{\operatorname{vol}(S)}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Therefore

\[ \mathbb E_t M_\theta(t) = \frac{ \operatorname{vol}\bigl(B_d\cap(B_d-\sigma(\theta))\bigr) }{ \operatorname{covol}(\Lambda) } \ge 4^{-d} \frac{\operatorname{vol}(B_d)}{\operatorname{covol}(\Lambda)}. \]

Using the point-count estimate above,

\[ \mathbb E_t M_\theta(t) \ge 4^{-d} \left(\frac{2\pi \ell^{2m}}{R_0}\right)^d. \]

Summing over all \(\theta\in\Theta\), and using

\[ |\Theta|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d, \]

we get

\[ \mathbb E_t I(t) \ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d 4^{-d} \left(\frac{2\pi \ell^{2m}}{R_0}\right)^d. \]

The right-hand side equals

\[ \left( \frac{(m+1)\pi \ell^{2m}}{2HR_0} \right)^d = L_m^d. \]

Therefore there exists \(t\) such that

\[ I(t)\ge L_m^d. \]

Fix such a \(t\), and set

\[ P:=P_t. \]

8. Unit-distance count

For every counted pair \((\alpha,\theta)\) contributing to \(I(t)\), both

\[ \sigma_1(\alpha) \quad\text{and}\quad \sigma_1(\alpha+\theta) \]

belong to \(P\), and their distance is

\[ |\sigma_1(\theta)|=1. \]

Thus \(I(t)\) counts directed unit-distance incidences in \(P\). Passing to unordered pairs can lose at most a factor of \(2\). Therefore

\[ u(P)\ge \frac12 I(t)\ge \frac12 L_m^d. \]

At the same time,

\[ |P|\le C_m^d. \]

Recall that \(m\) and \(\delta\) were chosen so that

\[ L_m>C_m^{1+\delta}. \]

Hence

\[ \frac12 L_m^d \ge C_m^{(1+\delta)d} \]

for all sufficiently large \(d\). Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(i\),

\[ u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

9. Growth of \(|P_i|\)

It remains only to check that \(|P_i|\to\infty\).

Since

\[ u(P_i)\ge \frac12 L_m^{d_i}, \]

and \(L_m>1\), the number of unit-distance pairs tends to infinity. But trivially

\[ u(P_i)\le \binom{|P_i|}{2}, \]

so \(|P_i|\to\infty\).

Discarding finitely many initial fields and relabeling gives an infinite sequence

\[ P_1,P_2,\dots\subset\mathbb R^2 \]

such that

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for every \(i\).

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

Summary of the mechanism

The construction works because of three simultaneous exponential effects:

  1. Many unit translations. In a CM field, every element of the form \(z/\overline z\) has modulus \(1\) in every complex embedding. Class-group pigeonholing among the ideals

    \[ \prod_j\mathfrak q_j^{e_j}, \qquad e_j\in\{0,\dots,m\}, \]

    gives at least

    \[ \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d \]

    distinct such translations.

  2. Polynomial-cost containment. All these translations lie inside the same fractional ideal

    \[ \ell^{-m}\mathcal O_K, \]

    whose covolume cost is only exponential in \(d\).

  3. Model-set overlap. In the full Minkowski embedding, every translation vector has length exactly \(\sqrt d\). A ball of radius \(\sqrt d\) therefore has overlap at least \(4^{-d}\) with each translate. This exponential loss is beaten by choosing \(m\) large enough.

The final parameters yield

\[ |P_i|\le C_m^{d_i} \]

but

\[ u(P_i)\ge \frac12 L_m^{d_i}, \]

with

\[ L_m>C_m^{1+\delta}. \]

Thus

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for a fixed absolute \(\delta>0\).

Proof

We identify \(\mathbb C\) with \(\mathbb R^2\).

1. A bounded-root-discriminant CM family with one completely split prime

Fix \(\ell=5\).

Choose \(20\) distinct rational primes

\[ r_1,\dots,r_{20}\equiv 1 \pmod {20}, \]

and put

\[ D=r_1\cdots r_{20}. \]

Then \(D\equiv 1\pmod 4\), so

\[ F_0=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D) \]

is a real quadratic field of discriminant \(D\). Also \(D\equiv 1\pmod 5\), so \(5\) splits in \(F_0\). Let \(T\) be the set of the two primes of \(F_0\) above \(5\).

By genus theory,

\[ d_2\operatorname{Cl}^+(F_0)=19, \]

where \(\operatorname{Cl}^+(F_0)\) is the narrow class group and

\[ d_2 A:=\dim_{\mathbb F_2} A/A^2. \]

Quotienting by the classes of the two primes in \(T\) lowers the \(2\)-rank by at most \(2\), so the \(T\)-split narrow \(2\)-class group has \(2\)-rank at least

\[ 19-2=17. \]

Now use the Golod–Shafarevich–Shafarevich theorem in the following standard form.

If \(F\) is a number field, \(T\) a finite set of finite primes, and \(G\) is the Galois group of the maximal pro-\(2\) extension of \(F\) unramified at all finite primes and split completely at all real places and at all primes of \(T\), then \(G\) has generator rank

\[ s=d_2\bigl(\operatorname{Cl}^+(F)/\langle T\rangle\bigr) \]

and relation rank at most

\[ s+r_1(F)+r_2(F)+|T|+1. \]

If this upper bound is \(<s^2/4\), then \(G\) is infinite.

For \(F_0\), we have \(r_1=2\), \(r_2=0\), \(|T|=2\), and \(s\ge 17\). Hence the relation rank is at most

\[ s+5, \]

while

\[ s+5<\frac{s^2}{4} \]

for all \(s\ge 17\). Therefore the \(T\)-split narrow \(2\)-class field tower of \(F_0\) is infinite.

Let

\[ F_1,F_2,\dots \]

be finite layers in this tower with

\[ d_i:=[F_i:\mathbb Q]\to\infty. \]

Then:

  • every \(F_i\) is totally real;
  • \(F_i/F_0\) is unramified at all finite primes;
  • every prime of \(T\) splits completely in \(F_i\);
  • therefore \(5\) splits completely in \(F_i\);
  • and
\[ \operatorname{rd}(F_i)=\operatorname{rd}(F_0)=\sqrt D. \]

Now define

\[ K_i:=F_i(i). \]

Since \(F_i\) is totally real, \(K_i\) is CM, with maximal totally real subfield \(F_i\). Also \(5\) splits in \(\mathbb Q(i)\), because \(5\equiv 1\pmod 4\). Hence \(5\) splits completely in \(K_i\).

\[ [K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d_i. \]

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le 2\operatorname{rd}(F_i)=2\sqrt D. \]

Set

Thus we have an infinite sequence of CM fields \(K_i\) with

\[ [K_i:\mathbb Q]=2d_i,\qquad d_i\to\infty, \]

such that

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_i)\le R_0 \]

and \(5\) splits completely in every \(K_i\).

2. A uniform exponential class-number bound

We need the following elementary estimate.

Lemma

There is a constant \(H\), depending only on \(R_0\), such that for every \(K_i\),

\[ h_{K_i}\le H^{d_i}. \]

Proof

Let \(K\) be one of the \(K_i\), and write

\[ [K:\mathbb Q]=2d. \]

Since \(K\) is totally imaginary, Minkowski’s bound says that every ideal class contains an integral ideal \(\mathfrak a\) with

\[ N\mathfrak a\le \left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^d \frac{(2d)!}{(2d)^{2d}} \sqrt{|\Delta_K|}. \]

Using \((2d)!\le (2d)^{2d}\) and

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_K|}\le R_0^d, \]

we get

\[ N\mathfrak a\le \left(\frac{4R_0}{\pi}\right)^d. \]

Let

\[ A:=\frac{4R_0}{\pi}. \]

Then every ideal class has a representative of norm at most \(A^d\).

Let \(I_K(X)\) be the number of integral ideals of \(K\) with norm at most \(X\). For \(X\ge 1\),

\[ I_K(X) \le X^2 \zeta_K(2). \]

Also,

\[ \zeta_K(2)\le \zeta(2)^{2d}. \]

Indeed, each Euler factor of \(\zeta_K(2)\) is bounded by the corresponding Euler factor of \(\zeta(2)^{2d}\).

Therefore

\[ h_K\le I_K(A^d) \le A^{2d}\zeta(2)^{2d}. \]

So it is enough to take

\[ H:=A^2\zeta(2)^2 = \left(\frac{4R_0}{\pi}\right)^2\zeta(2)^2. \]

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

3. Choice of parameters

For a positive integer \(m\), define

\[ C_m:=(1+2\cdot 5^m)^2 \]

We choose \(m\) so large that

Fix one of the fields \(K=K_i\), and write

\[ [K:\mathbb Q]=2d. \]

Let

\[ F=F_i \]

be its maximal totally real subfield.

corresponding to the \(d\) real embeddings of \(F\) and the choice \(i\mapsto i\). Thus

\[ \sigma_r(\overline x)=\overline{\sigma_r(x)} \]

for CM conjugation \(x\mapsto \overline x\).

Because \(5\) splits completely in \(K\), we may write

\[ 5\mathcal O_K = \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak q_j\overline{\mathfrak q_j}, \]

where each \(\mathfrak q_j\) has norm \(5\), and \(\overline{\mathfrak q_j}\) is its CM conjugate.

Let

\[ \mathcal E:=\{0,1,\dots,m\}^d. \]

For

\[ \mathbf e=(e_1,\dots,e_d)\in\mathcal E, \]

define

\[ \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}:=\prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak q_j^{e_j}. \]

The map

\[ \mathbf e\mapsto [\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}]\in\operatorname{Cl}(K) \]

takes \((m+1)^d\) values into a class group of size at most \(H^d\). Hence some fiber \(\mathcal F\subseteq\mathcal E\) has size at least

\[ |\mathcal F|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d. \]

Fix \(\mathbf e_*\in\mathcal F\).

For every \(\mathbf e\in\mathcal F\), the fractional ideal

\[ \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e_*}^{-1} \]

\[ (z_{\mathbf e}) = \mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e}\mathfrak Q_{\mathbf e_*}^{-1}. \]

Then

\[ \theta_{\mathbf e}\overline{\theta_{\mathbf e}}=1. \]

Therefore, for every \(r\),

\[ |\sigma_r(\theta_{\mathbf e})|=1. \]

In particular,

\[ |\sigma_1(\theta_{\mathbf e})|=1. \]

Also,

\[ (\theta_{\mathbf e}) = \prod_{j=1}^d \mathfrak q_j^{e_j-e_{*,j}} \overline{\mathfrak q_j}^{-(e_j-e_{*,j})}. \]

Since each exponent \(e_j-e_{*,j}\) lies between \(-m\) and \(m\), all finite valuations of \(\theta_{\mathbf e}\) are at least \(-m\) at primes above \(5\), and are nonnegative elsewhere. Hence

\[ \theta_{\mathbf e}\in 5^{-m}\mathcal O_K. \]

Finally, the \(\theta_{\mathbf e}\) are distinct: the valuation of \((\theta_{\mathbf e})\) at \(\mathfrak q_j\) is exactly \(e_j-e_{*,j}\), so the ideal \((\theta_{\mathbf e})\) determines \(\mathbf e\).

Thus the set

\[ \Theta:=\{\theta_{\mathbf e}:\mathbf e\in\mathcal F\} \]

satisfies

\[ |\Theta|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^d, \]

and every \(\theta\in\Theta\) lies in \(5^{-m}\mathcal O_K\) and has

\[ |\sigma_1(\theta)|=1. \]

\[ B_d:=\{x\in\mathbb C^d:\|x\|_2\le \sqrt d\}. \]

\[ |P_y|=|X_y|. \]

For \(\theta\in\Theta\), define

Therefore there exists some \(y\) such that

\[ I(y)\ge L_m^d. \]

\[ P:=P_y. \]

\[ |P|\le C_m^d. \]

Since

\[ L_m=C_m^{1+2\delta}, \]

\[ u(P) \ge \frac12 C_m^{(1+2\delta)d}. \]

For all sufficiently large \(d\),

\[ \frac12 C_m^{(1+2\delta)d} \ge C_m^{(1+\delta)d}. \]

Therefore, for all sufficiently large \(d\),

\[ u(P)\ge C_m^{(1+\delta)d}\ge |P|^{1+\delta}. \]

9. Forming the infinite sequence

Apply the construction to the fields \(K_i\). Since

\[ d_i\to\infty, \]

we may discard finitely many initial fields so that the final inequality holds for every remaining \(i\). Relabel the remaining point sets as

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for every \(i\).

\[ u(P_i)\ge \frac12 L_m^{d_i}\to\infty, \]

and always

\[ u(P_i)\le \binom{|P_i|}{2}. \]

Hence \(|P_i|\to\infty\).

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}. \]

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)
