System prompt

The provided theorem was recently proved, and the result is now accepted by the mathematical community. Your task is to independently construct a proof.

Round 1

User

Theorem. There exist an absolute constant $\delta > 0$ and an infinite sequence $\{P_i\}$ of finite point sets in $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that $|P_i| \to \infty$ and $u(P_i) \ge |P_i|^{1+\delta}$ for all $i$, where $u(P_i) = \frac{1}{2} | \{ (p,q) \in P_i \times P_i : \|p-q\|_2 = 1 \} |$ is the number of unit-distance pairs.

Remark. This result disproves the Erdős Unit Distance Conjecture.

As a first step, brainstorm and propose three most promising approaches to prove this theorem. Explore each approach to sufficient depth, and then rank them from most to least promising.

Assistant

Below are the three approaches I would pursue first. The common theme is that one should not try to beat the Erdős lattice construction inside an ordinary planar lattice. In a rank-2 lattice, the number of equal-length vectors is controlled by divisor-type bounds and is only \(n^{o(1)}\). The promising route is instead to use a high-rank additive structure projected into one complex embedding of a number field. Since the unit-distance problem imposes no separation condition, exponentially many algebraic points may live in a bounded region of \(\mathbb C \cong \mathbb R^2\).

I will first state a useful reduction, because all three approaches can be viewed through it.

Guiding reduction: many unit translations with small expansion

Identify \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\). Suppose we can find

\[ A \subset \mathbb C,\qquad D \subset S^1=\{z\in \mathbb C: |z|=1\}, \]

such that

\[ |A+D| \ll |A| \]

while \(|D|\) is a positive power of \(|A|\). Then

\[ P := A \cup (A+D) \]

has many unit distances: for every \(a\in A\) and \(d\in D\),

\[ a \longleftrightarrow a+d \]

is a unit-distance pair. Up to a harmless factor \(2\) from possible double-counting,

\[ u(P) \gtrsim |A|\,|D|. \]

If also

\[ |P| \le C|A|, \]

then

\[ u(P) \gtrsim |P|\,|D|. \]

Thus it suffices to arrange

\[ |D| \ge |P|^\delta \]

for some absolute \(\delta>0\). So the core problem becomes:

Construct a finite set \(A\subset \mathbb C\) that is almost invariant under many unit translations.

The three approaches below are different ways of producing such \(A,D\).

Approach 1: High-degree CM fields and norm-one \(S\)-units

Summary

This is the most promising approach. Use a tower of CM fields \(L_i\) of growing degree, with bounded root discriminant and a fixed rational prime \(p\) splitting completely. The norm-one \(p\)-\(S\)-units of \(L_i\) give exponentially many exact unit complex numbers under a chosen embedding. Height-bounded boxes of algebraic integers give large finite sets almost invariant under translation by all of them.

The construction is rigid enough to give exact unit distances, but flexible enough to produce polynomially many unit directions.

1.1. Why CM fields are natural

Let \(L\) be a CM field, i.e. a totally imaginary quadratic extension of a totally real field \(K\). Let

\[ \tau:L\to L \]

be complex conjugation, the nontrivial automorphism over \(K\). For every complex embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\),

\[ \sigma(\tau x)=\overline{\sigma(x)}. \]

Therefore, if

\[ u\tau(u)=1, \]

then

\[ |\sigma(u)|^2 = \sigma(u)\overline{\sigma(u)} = \sigma(u\tau(u)) = 1. \]

So every norm-one element \(u\in L^\times\) gives a unit complex number under any chosen embedding \(\sigma_0\):

\[ |\sigma_0(u)|=1. \]

Thus elements satisfying \(u\tau(u)=1\) are perfect unit directions.

1.2. Desired arithmetic input

We would like a sequence of CM fields \(L_i\) with

\[ [L_i:\mathbb Q]=2r_i\to \infty, \]

such that:

  1. The root discriminants are bounded:

    \[ \operatorname{rd}(L_i) := |\Delta_{L_i}|^{1/[L_i:\mathbb Q]} \le \rho \]

    for some absolute \(\rho\).

  2. A fixed rational prime \(p\) splits completely in every \(L_i\).

  3. The class groups are not too large. Bounded root discriminant gives an exponential upper bound

    \[ h_{L_i}\le H^{r_i} \]

    for some absolute \(H\).

Such towers are expected from, and in many settings can be built using, Golod–Shafarevich class-field-tower methods with prescribed splitting. One wants a CM class field tower in which a fixed prime \(p\) splits completely at every level.

1.3. Producing exponentially many norm-one unit directions

Fix one such CM field \(L\) of degree \(2r\). Because \(p\) splits completely,

\[ p\mathcal O_L = \prod_{j=1}^{r} \mathfrak p_j\, \tau(\mathfrak p_j), \]

where the primes above \(p\) come in conjugate pairs.

Choose an integer \(m\ge 1\), to be fixed later. For each vector

\[ e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)\in \{0,1,\dots,m\}^r, \]

define the ideal

\[ I_e := \prod_{j=1}^{r} \mathfrak p_j^{e_j}. \]

There are \((m+1)^r\) such ideals. Since there are at most \(h_L\le H^r\) ideal classes, by pigeonhole, if \(m\) is large enough, there is one ideal class containing at least

\[ \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r \]

of the \(I_e\)’s.

Fix one representative \(e^{(0)}\) from that large fiber. For every other \(e\) in the same fiber, the quotient ideal

\[ I_e I_{e^{(0)}}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose \(a_e\in L^\times\) with

\[ (a_e)=I_e I_{e^{(0)}}^{-1}. \]

Now define

\[ u_e := \frac{a_e}{\tau(a_e)}. \]

Then

\[ u_e\tau(u_e)=1, \]

so under any complex embedding \(\sigma_0\),

\[ |\sigma_0(u_e)|=1. \]

Thus the set

\[ D_L := \{\sigma_0(u_e)\} \subset S^1 \]

is a set of exact unit directions.

Distinct exponent vectors \(e\) give distinct valuation patterns, hence distinct \(u_e\). Therefore

\[ |D_L| \ge d^r \]

for some \(d>1\), after choosing \(m\) large enough.

Moreover, the valuations of \(u_e\) at primes above \(p\) are bounded between \(-m\) and \(m\). Hence

\[ q u_e\in \mathcal O_L \]

for

\[ q:=p^m. \]

Also, because \(u_e\tau(u_e)=1\),

\[ |\sigma(q u_e)|=q \]

for every embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\).

So we have exponentially many unit directions \(u_e\), all with uniformly bounded denominator \(q\) and uniformly bounded archimedean size after multiplying by \(q\).

This is the key miracle.

1.4. The finite point set

For \(T>0\), define a height box

\[ \mathcal B_L(T) := \{\alpha\in \mathcal O_L: |\sigma(\alpha)|\le T \text{ for every complex embedding } \sigma:L\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\}. \]

Fix a complex embedding \(\sigma_0:L\hookrightarrow \mathbb C\). Define

\[ P_L := \left\{ \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{q}\right) : \alpha\in \mathcal B_L(T+q) \right\} \subset \mathbb C\cong \mathbb R^2. \]

Also let

\[ A_L := \left\{ \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{q}\right) : \alpha\in \mathcal B_L(T) \right\} \subset P_L. \]

For every \(\alpha\in \mathcal B_L(T)\) and every \(u\in U_L:=\{u_e\}\), we have \(q u\in \mathcal O_L\) and

\[ |\sigma(\alpha+q u)| \le |\sigma(\alpha)|+|\sigma(q u)| \le T+q. \]

Thus

\[ \alpha+q u \in \mathcal B_L(T+q). \]

Therefore both points

\[ \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{q}\right) \quad\text{and}\quad \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha+q u}{q}\right) \]

lie in \(P_L\), and their difference is

\[ \sigma_0(u), \]

which has absolute value \(1\). Hence each pair \((\alpha,u)\) gives a unit distance.

So

\[ u(P_L) \gtrsim |\mathcal B_L(T)|\,|U_L|. \]

Since \(|U_L|\ge d^r\), this is

\[ u(P_L) \gtrsim |\mathcal B_L(T)|\,d^r. \]

1.5. Counting the height boxes

The Minkowski embedding of \(\mathcal O_L\) is a lattice in \(\mathbb C^r\) of covolume roughly

\[ 2^{-r}\sqrt{|\Delta_L|}. \]

Since the root discriminant is bounded,

\[ \sqrt{|\Delta_L|} = \operatorname{rd}(L)^r \le \rho^r. \]

The box \(\mathcal B_L(T)\) corresponds to a product of \(r\) disks of radius \(T\), with volume

\[ (\pi T^2)^r. \]

Therefore, for \(T\) sufficiently large depending only on \(\rho\),

\[ |\mathcal B_L(T)| \asymp_T c_T^r \]

for some base \(c_T>1\). Similarly,

\[ |\mathcal B_L(T+q)| \asymp_T c_{T+q}^r. \]

Thus

\[ |P_L| = |\mathcal B_L(T+q)| \le C_{T+q}^r, \]

while

\[ u(P_L) \gtrsim c_T^r d^r = (c_T d)^r. \]

By choosing \(m\) so that \(d>1\), then choosing \(T\gg q=p^m\) so that the enlargement loss from \(T\) to \(T+q\) is small, one can arrange

\[ c_T d > C_{T+q}. \]

Then there is some \(\delta>0\), depending only on \(p,m,T,\rho\), such that

\[ (c_T d)^r \ge (C_{T+q}^r)^{1+\delta}. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_L) \ge |P_L|^{1+\delta}. \]

Since \(r\to\infty\), we get an infinite sequence with \(|P_L|\to\infty\).

1.6. Why this approach is especially promising

This approach has several major advantages.

Exactness

The distances are exactly \(1\), not approximately \(1\). This comes from

\[ u\tau(u)=1. \]

Many directions

The number of directions is exponential in \(r\), coming from the completely split prime \(p\) and the large number of possible \(p\)-adic valuation patterns.

Small expansion

The height box \(\mathcal B_L(T)\) expands only to \(\mathcal B_L(T+q)\) after adding any of the unit directions. Since \(q\) is fixed while the degree grows, the expansion cost is only exponential with a fixed base, and it can be dominated by the exponential number of directions.

Avoids the lattice divisor barrier

In \(\mathbb Z^2\), the number of equal-length vectors is governed by divisor bounds. Here the underlying additive group has rank \(2r\), but it is projected into one complex embedding. This produces many exact unit translations in the plane without requiring many representations of an integer as a sum of two squares.

1.7. Main technical tasks

To complete this approach rigorously, the main ingredients to verify are:

  1. Existence of infinitely many CM fields \(L_i\) with bounded root discriminant and a fixed rational prime \(p\) splitting completely.
  2. A uniform exponential upper bound on class numbers \(h_{L_i}\).
  3. A clean pigeonhole argument in the \(p\)-split ideal lattice producing exponentially many distinct norm-one \(S\)-units of bounded denominator.
  4. Uniform lattice-counting estimates for the boxes \(\mathcal B_{L_i}(T)\).

Among the three approaches, this one gives the clearest route to a full proof.

Approach 2: Lift dense finite-field unit-distance graphs to the real plane

Summary

Over finite fields, the analogue of the theorem is easy and much stronger. In \(\mathbb F_q^2\), the unit-distance graph has \(q^2\) vertices and about \(q^3\) edges, i.e. \(N^{3/2}\) edges for \(N=q^2\). The idea is to lift a controlled portion of this finite-field construction to characteristic zero while preserving exact unit distances.

This is conceptually very attractive, but technically harder because finite-field equations can have accidental solutions that do not lift to real Euclidean configurations.

2.1. The finite-field model

Let \(q\) be odd, and define a graph \(G_q\) on \(\mathbb F_q^2\) by joining \(x,y\in \mathbb F_q^2\) if

\[ (x_1-y_1)^2+(x_2-y_2)^2=1. \]

For each \(x\), the number of \(y\) satisfying this equation is approximately \(q\). Therefore

\[ |V(G_q)|=q^2, \qquad |E(G_q)|\sim \frac12 q^3. \]

So the finite-field unit-distance graph already has the desired density with exponent \(3/2\).

In complex notation, if one works over \(\mathbb F_{q^2}\), then the unit circle is

\[ U_q = \{z\in \mathbb F_{q^2} : z z^q=1\}. \]

It has size \(q+1\), and the finite-field construction is simply

\[ \mathbb F_{q^2} \quad\text{with translations by}\quad U_q. \]

This is the finite-field version of the guiding reduction:

\[ A=\mathbb F_{q^2},\qquad D=U_q,\qquad A+D=A. \]

The expansion is zero because the ambient group is finite.

2.2. Naive lifting and the obstruction

One might try to lift the whole finite-field configuration to a number field \(L\) with a prime ideal \(\mathfrak p\) satisfying

\[ \mathcal O_L/\mathfrak p \cong \mathbb F_{q^2}. \]

Then one would lift the finite-field unit directions \(U_q\) to algebraic numbers \(u\) satisfying

\[ u\tau(u)=1, \]

so that their chosen complex embeddings are genuine unit complex numbers.

However, two major problems appear.

Problem 1: finite-field relations may be accidental

A cycle in the finite-field graph corresponds to an additive relation

\[ d_1+\cdots+d_k=0 \]

among finite-field unit directions \(d_j\in U_q\). Such a relation need not lift to an exact relation over \(\mathbb C\). It might hold only modulo \(\mathfrak p\).

Problem 2: clearing denominators may destroy the count

Even if each finite-field direction lifts individually, the common denominator or height needed to realize all lifted directions may be enormous. If the point set \(A\) must be made too large in order to be closed under those translations, the edge gain disappears.

This is exactly what happens in many naive cyclotomic or rational-parameter constructions: one gets many directions, but their arithmetic complexity is too large, and after clearing denominators the resulting exponent collapses back to \(1+o(1)\).

2.3. A refined lifting strategy

A more promising finite-field lifting strategy would proceed as follows.

  1. Choose a number field \(L\) with an involution \(\tau\), a chosen complex embedding \(\sigma_0\), and a prime \(\mathfrak p\) whose residue field is large.

  2. Lift a large subgroup of the finite-field norm-one group

    \[ \{z\in (\mathcal O_L/\mathfrak p)^\times : z\tau(z)=1\} \]

    to genuine norm-one algebraic elements

    \[ u\in L^\times,\qquad u\tau(u)=1. \]
  3. Ensure that all these \(u\)’s have bounded denominator and bounded height.

  4. Replace the finite group \(\mathbb F_{q^2}\) by a large archimedean Følner box of algebraic integers, such as

    \[ \mathcal B_L(T) = \{\alpha\in\mathcal O_L:|\sigma(\alpha)|\le T\text{ for all }\sigma\}. \]

  5. Use

    \[ \mathcal B_L(T)+qU\subseteq \mathcal B_L(T+q) \]

    to imitate the finite-field identity \(A+D=A\).

This is essentially a bridge from the finite-field construction to the CM \(S\)-unit construction in Approach 1.

2.4. Why this approach is promising

The finite-field picture tells us exactly what density we should be trying to emulate:

\[ N^{3/2}. \]

It also suggests the correct algebraic objects:

  • norm-one elements,
  • split primes,
  • residue fields,
  • finite additive groups,
  • bounded-denominator lifts,
  • Følner boxes replacing finite groups.

So even if the direct finite-field lift is technically dangerous, it is an excellent conceptual guide.

2.5. Main difficulties

The key obstacles are:

  1. Realness. Lifting a solution of

    \[ x^2+y^2=1 \]

    over a finite field does not automatically give a real Euclidean unit vector.

  2. Exact additive relations. Finite-field relations may not lift to characteristic zero.
  3. Height explosion. Lifts may require denominators or coefficients too large to preserve a polynomial edge gain.
  4. Loss of periodicity. Finite fields have \(A+D=A\) exactly. Over \(\mathbb R^2\), finite sets cannot be truly periodic, so one must use Følner boxes and control boundary growth.

For these reasons, I rank this below the CM \(S\)-unit construction, which directly solves the exactness and realness issues.

Approach 3: Incidence/additive-combinatorial construction via rich unit-circle incidences

Summary

A third approach is to attack the problem directly as an incidence problem. A unit distance between \(p\) and \(q\) is the same as an incidence between \(q\) and the unit circle centered at \(p\). Thus one wants a set of centers \(C\) and a set of query points \(Q\) such that many unit circles centered at points of \(C\) pass through points of \(Q\). Equivalently, one wants many unit translations from \(C\) into \(Q\).

This approach is broad and flexible, but without extra arithmetic input it runs into strong expansion phenomena for the unit circle.

3.1. Incidence formulation

Let

\[ C,Q\subset \mathbb R^2. \]

The number of unit-distance pairs from \(C\) to \(Q\) is

\[ I(C,Q) = |\{(c,q)\in C\times Q: \|c-q\|_2=1\}|. \]

Equivalently,

\[ I(C,Q) = |\{(c,q): q\in c+S^1\}|. \]

If

\[ P=C\cup Q, \]

then

\[ u(P)\ge \frac12 I(C,Q). \]

So one can try to build \(C,Q\) with

\[ I(C,Q)\ge |C\cup Q|^{1+\delta}. \]

The guiding reduction corresponds to taking

\[ Q=C+D \]

for many unit vectors \(D\subset S^1\).

3.2. Additive-combinatorial target

A purely additive-combinatorial version is:

Find \(C\subset \mathbb R^2\) and \(D\subset S^1\) such that

\[ |C+D|\le K|C| \]

with \(|D|\) much larger than \(K\).

Indeed, if

\[ Q=C+D, \]

then

\[ I(C,Q)\ge |C||D|, \]

while

\[ |P|\le |C|+|C+D|\le (K+1)|C|. \]

Thus

\[ u(P)\gtrsim |C||D|. \]

To get a superlinear power, one wants

\[ |D|\ge |C|^\delta \]

while \(K\) stays bounded, or at least grows more slowly than \(|D|\).

This is the same small-expansion framework as before, but one might try to build \(C,D\) directly using real additive combinatorics rather than algebraic number theory.

3.3. Cartesian-product variant

A particularly concrete variant is to take

\[ P=A\times A \]

for some finite \(A\subset \mathbb R\). Then

\[ 2u(P) = |\{(a,a',b,b')\in A^4: (a-a')^2+(b-b')^2=1\}|. \]

Equivalently,

\[ 2u(P) = \sum_{\substack{x,y\in A-A\\ x^2+y^2=1}} r_{A-A}(x)\,r_{A-A}(y), \]

where

\[ r_{A-A}(x) = |\{(a,a')\in A^2:a-a'=x\}|. \]

So one wants many Pythagorean pairs \((x,y)\in S^1\) such that both \(x\) and \(y\) are highly popular differences of \(A\).

If one could find \(A\) with \(|A|=M\) and \(K\) unit directions \((x_j,y_j)\) such that

\[ r_{A-A}(x_j), r_{A-A}(y_j) \gg M \]

for all \(j\), then

\[ u(A\times A)\gg K M^2. \]

Since

\[ |A\times A|=M^2, \]

we would get

\[ u(P)\gg |P|\cdot K. \]

Thus \(K\ge M^{2\delta}\) would suffice.

This formulation reduces the problem to building a one-dimensional set \(A\) with many popular Pythagorean differences.

3.4. Possible arithmetic source for such \(A\)

One possible route is again algebraic. Suppose \(A\) is the projection under a chosen embedding of a height-bounded box in a number field. Then many algebraic numbers of bounded denominator can act as popular differences of \(A\). If, moreover, many pairs of such differences satisfy

\[ x^2+y^2=1, \]

one obtains many unit distances in \(A\times A\).

In a CM field, a norm-one element \(u\) gives

\[ u+\tau(u),\qquad \frac{u-\tau(u)}{i} \]

as real and imaginary parts. These satisfy the unit-circle equation. So one could try to make the real and imaginary parts of many norm-one \(S\)-units simultaneously popular differences of a real set \(A\).

This is less clean than Approach 1 because one must control two projections separately, but it is a plausible alternate route.

3.5. Algebraic-torus formulation

Another version is to use algebraic tori.

The unit circle is a real form of the multiplicative torus:

\[ S^1=\{z\in \mathbb C^\times : z\overline z=1\}. \]

If one can find a high-dimensional algebraic torus \(T\), a finite torsion or \(S\)-unit subset \(X\subset T\), and a map

\[ \Phi:T\to \mathbb C \]

such that for many parameters \(\lambda\),

\[ \Phi(\lambda x)-\Phi(x)\in S^1 \]

for many \(x\in X\), then finite grids in \(T\) could produce many unit distances after projection to \(\mathbb C\).

This would be an algebraic-geometric version of the same idea: many transformations of a high-dimensional parameter space induce unit translations in the plane.

3.6. Obstacles

This approach faces serious structural barriers.

Expansion of convex curves

Sets on strictly convex curves usually expand additively. For many \(D\subset S^1\), one expects

\[ |D+D| \]

to be large. Thus obtaining a large unit direction set \(D\) with small additive complexity is hard.

Fixed curves give only linear unit chords

If \(P\) lies on a fixed irreducible algebraic curve, then each point usually has only \(O(1)\) other points at unit distance on that curve. Thus one needs a genuinely high-rank construction, not just many points on one curve.

Hidden group structure is necessary

Elekes–Szabó type phenomena suggest that large Cartesian-product intersections with an algebraic relation usually require group-like structure. Therefore, a successful incidence/additive-combinatorial construction probably needs to smuggle in an algebraic group, such as the norm-one torus in a CM field. That again points back toward Approach 1.

Ranking

1. Most promising: CM fields and norm-one \(S\)-units

This is the strongest candidate. It gives an explicit mechanism:

  • norm-one elements give exact unit directions;
  • split primes give exponentially many such directions;
  • bounded root discriminant gives exponentially large height boxes;
  • height boxes expand only mildly under all those translations.

It turns the geometric problem into a controlled arithmetic construction, and the estimates naturally produce

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for some absolute \(\delta>0\).

This is the approach I would try to turn into a full proof first.

2. Second most promising: finite-field lifting

Finite fields have the perfect model construction:

\[ q^2 \text{ vertices},\qquad \sim q^3 \text{ unit edges}. \]

This gives the right intuition and suggests the right algebraic objects. However, direct lifting to \(\mathbb R^2\) is technically treacherous because of accidental finite-field relations, height explosion, and realness issues.

I would use this approach mainly as a conceptual guide. A refined version of it essentially leads to the CM \(S\)-unit strategy.

3. Third most promising: incidence/additive-combinatorial construction

This is the broadest and most flexible framework. It asks directly for finite sets \(C,D\) with

\[ D\subset S^1,\qquad |C+D|\ll |C|,\qquad |D|\ge |C|^\delta. \]

If achieved, the theorem follows immediately. But without an arithmetic source of many low-complexity unit directions, expansion phenomena on the circle make this difficult.

Thus I would keep this as a useful reformulation and possible source of alternative constructions, but I would not prioritize it over the CM \(S\)-unit route.

Round 2

User

Thoroughly develop your top-ranked approach to construct a complete and rigorous proof, adapting your strategy as needed.

Assistant

We identify \(\mathbb R^2\) with \(\mathbb C\). I will give a full construction following the CM-field/\(S\)-unit strategy, but I will make one important adaptation to the brainstorm: instead of merely assuming a tower of CM fields with bounded root discriminant and a fixed split prime, I will build such a tower by starting from a totally real class-field tower in which \(5\) splits completely, and then adjoining \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). This gives CM fields \(L=K(i)\) with a canonical complex conjugation \(\tau\), and \(5\) splits completely in every \(L\).

The proof has one standard deep input from class-field-tower theory, namely Golod-Shafarevich with prescribed splitting. Everything after that is elementary geometry of numbers and ideal arithmetic.

1. A split CM tower of bounded root discriminant

We first construct CM fields \(L_j\) of degrees tending to infinity, with uniformly bounded root discriminant, and with one fixed rational prime split completely in all of them.

We will use the prime

\[ p=5. \]

The reason for choosing \(5\) is that \(5\equiv 1\pmod 4\), so it splits in \(\mathbb Q(i)\).

1.1. A totally real tower with \(5\) split completely

We need the following standard class-field-tower fact.

Lemma 1.1: A totally real split tower

There exists an infinite sequence of totally real number fields

\[ K_1,K_2,K_3,\dots \]

such that

\[ [K_j:\mathbb Q]\to\infty, \]

the root discriminants \(\operatorname{rd}(K_j)\) are bounded independently of \(j\), and the rational prime \(5\) splits completely in every \(K_j\).

Proof

Choose a large integer \(t\). Let

\[ \ell_1,\dots,\ell_t \]

be distinct rational primes satisfying

\[ \ell_a\equiv 1\pmod {20}. \]

Such primes exist by Dirichlet’s theorem.

Set

\[ D:=\ell_1\ell_2\cdots \ell_t \]

and

\[ K_0:=\mathbb Q(\sqrt D). \]

Then \(K_0\) is real quadratic. Since every \(\ell_a\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the discriminant of \(K_0\) is \(D\). Also, since every \(\ell_a\equiv 1\pmod 5\), we have

\[ D\equiv 1\pmod 5, \]

so \(D\) is a quadratic residue modulo \(5\). Therefore \(5\) splits in \(K_0\). Let

\[ S=\{\mathfrak q_1,\mathfrak q_2\} \]

be the two primes of \(K_0\) above \(5\).

Now let \(K_\infty/K_0\) be the maximal pro-\(2\) extension satisfying the following local conditions:

  1. it is unramified at all finite primes;
  2. both real places of \(K_0\) split completely, so all finite subextensions are totally real;
  3. the two primes \(\mathfrak q_1,\mathfrak q_2\) above \(5\) split completely.

Let

\[ G:=\operatorname{Gal}(K_\infty/K_0). \]

We claim that for \(t\) sufficiently large, \(G\) is infinite.

By genus theory for real quadratic fields, the narrow \(2\)-class rank of \(K_0\) is

\[ \dim_{\mathbb F_2} \operatorname{Cl}^+(K_0)/2\operatorname{Cl}^+(K_0) = t-1. \]

Imposing that the two primes above \(5\) split completely amounts, on the maximal elementary abelian \(2\)-quotient, to quotienting by the subgroup generated by the ideal classes of those two primes. Hence the generator rank \(d(G)\) satisfies

\[ d(G)\ge t-3. \]

On the other hand, Shafarevich’s relation-rank estimate for this split, everywhere-unramified pro-\(2\) extension gives

\[ r(G)\le d(G)+6. \]

The precise additive constant is irrelevant; the key point is that it is absolute here because \(K_0\) has fixed signature and we impose splitting at only two finite primes.

The Golod-Shafarevich inequality says that if a finitely generated pro-\(2\) group \(G\) is finite, then

\[ r(G)>\frac{d(G)^2}{4}. \]

Choosing \(t\) large enough so that

\[ d+6\le \frac{d^2}{4} \]

for every \(d\ge t-3\), we get a contradiction. Therefore \(G\) is infinite.

Now choose finite subextensions

\[ K_0\subset K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots\subset K_\infty \]

with

\[ [K_j:K_0]\to\infty. \]

Because \(K_\infty/K_0\) is unramified at all finite primes, every \(K_j/K_0\) is unramified at all finite primes. Therefore

\[ \operatorname{rd}(K_j)=\operatorname{rd}(K_0)=\sqrt D. \]

Because the real places split completely, every \(K_j\) is totally real. Because the primes above \(5\) split completely in \(K_\infty/K_0\), and \(5\) already splits in \(K_0\), the rational prime \(5\) splits completely in every \(K_j\).

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

1.2. Passing to CM fields

For each \(j\), define

\[ L_j:=K_j(i). \]

Since \(K_j\) is totally real, \(L_j\) is a CM field. Let

\[ \tau_j:L_j\to L_j \]

be the nontrivial automorphism over \(K_j\), i.e.

\[ \tau_j(i)=-i. \]

Thus \(L_j/K_j\) is a totally imaginary quadratic extension of the totally real field \(K_j\).

Let

\[ r_j:=[K_j:\mathbb Q]. \]

Then

\[ [L_j:\mathbb Q]=2r_j \]

and \(r_j\to\infty\).

Because \(5\) splits completely in \(K_j\), and because \(5\equiv 1\pmod 4\), the polynomial \(x^2+1\) splits modulo every prime of \(K_j\) above \(5\). Therefore \(5\) splits completely in \(L_j\). Thus

\[ 5\mathcal O_{L_j} = \prod_{a=1}^{r_j} \mathfrak p_a\,\tau_j(\mathfrak p_a), \]

where the \(2r_j\) primes above \(5\) are paired by the CM involution \(\tau_j\).

Finally, the root discriminants of \(L_j\) are bounded. Indeed, the relative discriminant of \(K_j(i)/K_j\) divides \(4\mathcal O_{K_j}\), since \(\mathcal O_{K_j}[i]\) has relative discriminant dividing \(4\). Hence

\[ |\Delta_{L_j}| \le |\Delta_{K_j}|^2\cdot 4^{[K_j:\mathbb Q]}. \]

Taking \(1/[L_j:\mathbb Q]=1/(2r_j)\)-th powers gives

\[ \operatorname{rd}(L_j) \le 2\,\operatorname{rd}(K_j) = 2\sqrt D. \]

Set

\[ \rho:=2\sqrt D. \]

We have proved the following.

Proposition 1.2: Split CM tower

There exists a sequence of CM fields \(L_j\) with CM involutions \(\tau_j\), degrees

\[ [L_j:\mathbb Q]=2r_j,\qquad r_j\to\infty, \]

such that

\[ \operatorname{rd}(L_j)\le \rho \]

for a fixed absolute constant \(\rho\), and such that the prime \(5\) splits completely in every \(L_j\).

2. Uniform counting and class-number estimates

We now record two uniform estimates that will be used for every field \(L_j\).

Let \(L\) be a CM field of degree \(2r\), with

\[ \operatorname{rd}(L)\le \rho. \]

Fix one embedding from each conjugate pair,

\[ \Sigma=\{\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_r\}, \]

so the Minkowski embedding is

\[ \Phi:L\hookrightarrow \mathbb C^r,\qquad \Phi(\alpha)=(\sigma_1(\alpha),\dots,\sigma_r(\alpha)). \]

For \(R>0\), define the archimedean box

\[ \mathcal B_L(R) := \left\{ \alpha\in\mathcal O_L: |\sigma(\alpha)|\le R \text{ for every }\sigma\in\Sigma \right\}. \]

Since the omitted embeddings are just complex conjugates, this is equivalent to requiring all complex conjugates of \(\alpha\) to have absolute value at most \(R\).

2.1. Counting algebraic integers of bounded conjugates

Lemma 2.1

For every \(R>0\),

\[ |\mathcal B_L(R)| \ge \left(\frac{\pi R^2}{2\rho}\right)^r. \]

Also,

\[ |\mathcal B_L(R)| \le (2R+1)^{2r}. \]

Proof

The lattice \(\Phi(\mathcal O_L)\subset\mathbb C^r\cong\mathbb R^{2r}\) has covolume

\[ 2^{-r}\sqrt{|\Delta_L|}. \]

The body

\[ C_R:=\{z=(z_1,\dots,z_r)\in\mathbb C^r: |z_a|\le R\text{ for all }a\} \]

has volume

\[ \operatorname{vol}(C_R)=(\pi R^2)^r. \]

We use the following standard van der Corput/Blichfeldt estimate: if \(\Lambda\) is a lattice in \(\mathbb R^n\) and \(C\) is a centrally symmetric convex body, then

\[ |\Lambda\cap C| \ge \frac{\operatorname{vol}(C)}{2^n\det \Lambda}. \]

Here \(n=2r\). Therefore

\[ |\mathcal B_L(R)| = |\Phi(\mathcal O_L)\cap C_R| \ge \frac{(\pi R^2)^r}{2^{2r}\cdot 2^{-r}\sqrt{|\Delta_L|}} = \left( \frac{\pi R^2}{2\operatorname{rd}(L)} \right)^r \ge \left( \frac{\pi R^2}{2\rho} \right)^r. \]

This proves the lower bound.

For the upper bound, let

\[ k:=\lfloor 2R\rfloor+1. \]

Then \(k>2R\). Suppose \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal B_L(R)\) and

\[ \alpha\equiv \beta\pmod{k\mathcal O_L}. \]

Set \(\gamma=\alpha-\beta\). If \(\gamma\neq 0\), then \(\gamma=k\eta\) for some nonzero \(\eta\in\mathcal O_L\), so

\[ |N_{L/\mathbb Q}(\gamma)| \ge k^{2r}. \]

On the other hand, for every embedding \(\sigma\),

\[ |\sigma(\gamma)| \le |\sigma(\alpha)|+|\sigma(\beta)| \le 2R. \]

Thus

\[ |N_{L/\mathbb Q}(\gamma)| \le (2R)^{2r} < k^{2r}, \]

a contradiction. Hence \(\gamma=0\), so reduction modulo \(k\mathcal O_L\) is injective on \(\mathcal B_L(R)\). Therefore

\[ |\mathcal B_L(R)| \le |\mathcal O_L/k\mathcal O_L| = k^{2r} \le (2R+1)^{2r}. \]

This proves the lemma. \(\square\)

2.2. A uniform class-number bound

We also need a uniform exponential upper bound for class numbers.

Lemma 2.2

There exists a constant \(H\), depending only on \(\rho\), such that for every CM field \(L\) of degree \(2r\) with \(\operatorname{rd}(L)\le\rho\),

\[ h_L\le H^r. \]

Proof

By Minkowski’s ideal theorem, every ideal class of \(L\) contains an integral ideal \(\mathfrak a\) satisfying

\[ N\mathfrak a \le \left(\frac{4}{\pi}\right)^r \frac{(2r)!}{(2r)^{2r}} \sqrt{|\Delta_L|} \le \left(\frac{4\rho}{\pi}\right)^r. \]

Let

\[ X:=\left(\frac{4\rho}{\pi}\right)^r. \]

The number of integral ideals of norm at most \(X\) is at most

\[ X^2\zeta_L(2). \]

Since

\[ \zeta_L(2)\le \zeta(2)^{2r}, \]

we get

\[ h_L \le X^2\zeta_L(2) \le \left(\frac{4\rho}{\pi}\right)^{2r} \zeta(2)^{2r}. \]

Thus it suffices to take

\[ H:= \max\left\{ 2, \left(\frac{4\rho\,\zeta(2)}{\pi}\right)^2 \right\}. \]

Then

\[ h_L\le H^r. \]

\(\square\)

3. Many norm-one \(S\)-units of bounded denominator

Now fix one field \(L=L_j\) from the CM tower. Write

\[ [L:\mathbb Q]=2r, \]

and let \(\tau\) be its CM involution.

Since \(5\) splits completely in \(L\), we may write

\[ 5\mathcal O_L = \prod_{a=1}^r \mathfrak p_a\,\tau(\mathfrak p_a), \]

where the \(2r\) primes above \(5\) are grouped into \(r\) conjugate pairs.

Let \(m\ge 1\) be a positive integer, to be chosen later. For each vector

\[ e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}^r, \]

define the ideal

\[ I_e := \prod_{a=1}^r \mathfrak p_a^{e_a}. \]

There are \((m+1)^r\) such ideals. Since \(h_L\le H^r\), some ideal class contains at least

\[ \frac{(m+1)^r}{H^r} = \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r \]

of them. Let \(E\subseteq \{0,1,\dots,m\}^r\) be such a subset, all of whose ideals \(I_e\) lie in the same ideal class. Thus

\[ |E|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r. \]

Fix some \(e^{(0)}\in E\). For each \(e\in E\), the fractional ideal

\[ I_e I_{e^{(0)}}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose \(a_e\in L^\times\) such that

\[ (a_e)=I_e I_{e^{(0)}}^{-1}. \]

Define

\[ w_e:=\frac{a_e}{\tau(a_e)}. \]

Then

\[ w_e\tau(w_e)=1. \]

Therefore, for every complex embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\),

\[ |\sigma(w_e)|=1. \]

Indeed, because \(L\) is CM,

\[ \sigma(\tau x)=\overline{\sigma(x)} \]

for every \(x\in L\). Hence

\[ |\sigma(w_e)|^2 = \sigma(w_e)\overline{\sigma(w_e)} = \sigma(w_e)\sigma(\tau w_e) = \sigma(w_e\tau w_e) = 1. \]

Now compute the principal ideal of \(w_e\). We get

\[ (w_e) = \frac{(a_e)}{(\tau a_e)} = \prod_{a=1}^r \mathfrak p_a^{e_a-e_a^{(0)}} \tau(\mathfrak p_a)^{-(e_a-e_a^{(0)})}. \]

Thus distinct exponent vectors \(e\) give distinct principal ideals \((w_e)\), hence distinct elements \(w_e\).

Moreover, because each difference \(e_a-e_a^{(0)}\) lies between \(-m\) and \(m\), multiplication by

\[ q:=5^m \]

clears all denominators:

\[ q w_e\in\mathcal O_L. \]

Indeed, \(q=5^m\) has valuation \(m\) at every prime above \(5\), and the negative valuations of \(w_e\) are at worst \(-m\).

Therefore we have constructed a set

\[ W_L:=\{w_e:e\in E\}\subset L^\times \]

with the following properties:

  1. \(|W_L|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r\);
  2. \(w\tau(w)=1\) for every \(w\in W_L\);
  3. \(q w\in\mathcal O_L\) for every \(w\in W_L\);

  4. for every embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\),

    \[ |\sigma(w)|=1. \]

The elements of \(W_L\) will be our unit directions.

4. Choosing fixed parameters

We now choose \(m,q,T,R\), once and for all.

Recall that \(H\) and \(\rho\) are fixed constants. Choose \(m\) so large that

\[ \frac{m+1}{H} > \frac{36\rho}{\pi}. \]

Set

\[ \mu:=\frac{m+1}{H}. \]

Thus

\[ \mu>\frac{36\rho}{\pi}. \]

Set

\[ q:=5^m. \]

Now choose

\[ T:=2q+1, \qquad R:=T+q=3q+1. \]

Define two constants

\[ A:=\frac{\pi T^2}{2\rho}, \qquad B:=(2R+1)^2. \]

Because \(R=3q+1\) and \(T=2q+1\),

\[ 2R+1=6q+3=3T, \]

so

\[ B=9T^2. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{B}{A} = \frac{9T^2}{\pi T^2/(2\rho)} = \frac{18\rho}{\pi}. \]

Since \(\mu>36\rho/\pi\), we have

\[ \frac{A\mu}{B} > 2. \]

In particular,

\[ A\mu>B. \]

Define

\[ \eta := \frac{\log(A\mu/B)}{\log B} >0. \]

Finally set

\[ \delta := \min\left\{ \frac12,\frac{\eta}{2} \right\}. \]

This is a positive absolute constant. It depends only on the fixed tower and the fixed choice of \(m\), not on \(j\).

5. The point sets

Now fix \(L=L_j\), with degree \(2r\), where \(r=r_j\).

Choose one complex embedding

\[ \sigma_0:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C. \]

Define

\[ P_L := \left\{ \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{q}\right) : \alpha\in\mathcal B_L(R) \right\} \subset\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2. \]

Since \(\sigma_0\) is injective, distinct \(\alpha\)'s give distinct points. Hence

\[ |P_L| = |\mathcal B_L(R)|. \]

By Lemma 2.1,

\[ |P_L| \le (2R+1)^{2r} = B^r. \]

Also,

\[ |\mathcal B_L(T)| \ge \left(\frac{\pi T^2}{2\rho}\right)^r = A^r. \]

Since \(A>1\) and \(r_j\to\infty\), we will have \(|P_{L_j}|\to\infty\).

6. Counting unit distances

Let \(W_L\) be the set of norm-one \(S\)-units constructed above. We have

\[ |W_L|\ge \mu^r. \]

Take any

\[ \alpha\in\mathcal B_L(T) \]

and any

\[ w\in W_L. \]

Because \(q w\in\mathcal O_L\), the element

\[ \beta:=\alpha+qw \]

is an algebraic integer.

For every embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\),

\[ |\sigma(\beta)| \le |\sigma(\alpha)|+q|\sigma(w)| \le T+q = R. \]

Thus

\[ \beta\in\mathcal B_L(R). \]

So both

\[ x:=\sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{q}\right) \]

and

\[ y:=\sigma_0\left(\frac{\beta}{q}\right) = \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha+qw}{q}\right) \]

belong to \(P_L\).

Their difference is

\[ y-x = \sigma_0(w). \]

Since \(|\sigma_0(w)|=1\), this is a unit-distance pair.

Thus every pair

\[ (\alpha,w)\in \mathcal B_L(T)\times W_L \]

produces an ordered unit-distance pair in \(P_L\).

These ordered pairs are distinct. Indeed, if two pairs \((\alpha,w)\) and \((\alpha',w')\) produce the same ordered pair \((x,y)\), then

\[ \sigma_0(\alpha/q)=\sigma_0(\alpha'/q), \]

so \(\alpha=\alpha'\). Then

\[ \sigma_0(w)=y-x=\sigma_0(w'), \]

so \(w=w'\).

Therefore the number of ordered unit-distance pairs in \(P_L\) is at least

\[ |\mathcal B_L(T)|\,|W_L|. \]

Since \(u(P_L)\) is half the number of ordered unit-distance pairs, we get

\[ u(P_L) \ge \frac12 |\mathcal B_L(T)|\,|W_L|. \]

Using the bounds above,

\[ u(P_L) \ge \frac12 A^r\mu^r = \frac12(A\mu)^r. \]

But by definition of \(\eta\),

\[ A\mu = B^{1+\eta}. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_L) \ge \frac12 B^{(1+\eta)r}. \]

Since \(\delta\le \eta/2\), we have \(\eta-\delta\ge\eta/2>0\). Hence for all sufficiently large \(r\),

\[ \frac12 B^{(1+\eta)r} \ge B^{(1+\delta)r}. \]

Thus, for all sufficiently large \(j\),

\[ u(P_{L_j}) \ge B^{(1+\delta)r_j}. \]

On the other hand,

\[ |P_{L_j}|\le B^{r_j}. \]

Therefore

\[ u(P_{L_j}) \ge |P_{L_j}|^{1+\delta} \]

for all sufficiently large \(j\).

Discard finitely many initial fields and relabel the remaining point sets as

\[ P_1,P_2,P_3,\dots. \]

Then

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for every \(i\).

This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

7. Summary of the mechanism

The construction works because the high-degree CM fields supply many exact unit directions at low denominator.

The crucial points are:

  1. Exact unit directions. If \(w\tau(w)=1\), then for every embedding \(\sigma\),

    \[ |\sigma(w)|=1. \]

  2. Many such directions. Because \(5\) splits completely in \(L\), the primes above \(5\) come in \(r\) conjugate pairs. Exponent vectors

    \[ e\in\{0,\dots,m\}^r \]

    give many ideals. Pigeonholing in the class group produces exponentially many principal quotients, hence exponentially many \(w\)'s satisfying \(w\tau(w)=1\).

  3. Bounded denominator. All those directions satisfy

    \[ 5^m w\in\mathcal O_L. \]

    Thus translating an algebraic-integer box by \(5^m w\) keeps us inside a slightly larger box.

  4. Small expansion. The boxes

    \[ \mathcal B_L(T) \subset \mathcal B_L(R) \]

    have sizes both exponential in \(r\), with controlled bases.

  5. Polynomial gain. The number of unit directions is also exponential in \(r\). Since the total point count is exponential in \(r\), this exponential directional gain becomes a power gain

    \[ |P|^\delta \]

    in the number of unit distances.

Thus the final point sets satisfy

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for some fixed \(\delta>0\), as required.

\[ e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}^r, \]

define

\[ I_e:=\prod_{a=1}^r \mathfrak p_a^{e_a}. \]

There are \((m+1)^r\) such ideals. Since \(h_L\le H^r\), some ideal class contains at least

\[ \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r \]

of them. Let \(E\subseteq\{0,\dots,m\}^r\) be such a set. Fix \(e^{(0)}\in E\).

For every \(e\in E\), the fractional ideal

\[ I_eI_{e^{(0)}}^{-1} \]

is principal. Choose \(a_e\in L^\times\) with

\[ (a_e)=I_eI_{e^{(0)}}^{-1}. \]

Define

\[ w_e:=\frac{a_e}{\tau(a_e)}. \]

Then

\[ w_e\tau(w_e)=1. \]

Therefore, for every complex embedding \(\sigma:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C\),

\[ |\sigma(w_e)|=1. \]

Indeed, because \(\tau\) is the CM involution,

\[ \sigma(\tau x)=\overline{\sigma(x)}. \]

Thus

\[ |\sigma(w_e)|^2 = \sigma(w_e)\overline{\sigma(w_e)} = \sigma(w_e\tau(w_e)) = 1. \]

The principal ideal of \(w_e\) is

\[ (w_e) = \prod_{a=1}^r \mathfrak p_a^{e_a-e_a^{(0)}} \tau(\mathfrak p_a)^{-(e_a-e_a^{(0)})}. \]

Hence distinct \(e\)'s give distinct \(w_e\)'s.

Let

\[ W_L:=\{w_e:e\in E\}. \]

Then

\[ |W_L|\ge \left(\frac{m+1}{H}\right)^r. \]

Also, if

\[ Q:=5^m, \]

then

\[ Qw_e\in\mathcal O_L \]

for every \(e\in E\), because every negative valuation of \(w_e\) at a prime over \(5\) has size at most \(m\).

5. Choosing constants

Choose \(m\) so large that, with

\[ \mu:=\frac{m+1}{H}, \]

we have

\[ \mu>\frac{36\rho}{\pi}. \]

Also choose \(m\) large enough that

\[ \frac{\pi(3\cdot 5^m+1)^2}{2\rho}>1. \]

Set

\[ Q:=5^m, \qquad T:=2Q+1, \qquad R:=3Q+1. \]

Define

\[ a_0:=\frac{\pi T^2}{2\rho}, \qquad b_0:=(2R+1)^2. \]

Since

\[ 2R+1=6Q+3=3(2Q+1)=3T, \]

we have

\[ b_0=9T^2. \]

Therefore

\[ \frac{b_0}{a_0} = \frac{9T^2}{\pi T^2/(2\rho)} = \frac{18\rho}{\pi}. \]

Our choice of \(m\) gives

\[ \frac{a_0\mu}{b_0}>2. \]

Define

\[ \eta:= \frac{\log(a_0\mu/b_0)}{\log b_0}>0. \]

Then

\[ a_0\mu=b_0^{1+\eta}. \]

Finally set

\[ \delta:=\frac{\eta}{2}>0. \]

This \(\delta\) is an absolute constant: it depends only on the fixed tower construction and the fixed integer \(m\), not on \(j\).

6. The point sets

For \(L=L_j\), choose one complex embedding

\[ \sigma_0:L\hookrightarrow\mathbb C. \]

Define

\[ P_L := \left\{ \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{Q}\right): \alpha\in\mathcal C_L(R) \right\} \subset\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2. \]

Because \(\sigma_0\) is injective,

\[ |P_L|=|\mathcal C_L(R)|. \]

By the upper lattice-counting bound,

\[ |P_L| \le (2R+1)^{2r} = b_0^r. \]

By the lower bound,

\[ |\mathcal C_L(T)| \ge \left(\frac{\pi T^2}{2\rho}\right)^r = a_0^r. \]

Also, since \(\pi R^2/(2\rho)>1\), we have

\[ |P_L|\ge \left(\frac{\pi R^2}{2\rho}\right)^r\to\infty \]

as \(r=r_j\to\infty\).

7. Counting unit distances

Take

\[ \alpha\in\mathcal C_L(T), \qquad w\in W_L. \]

Since \(Qw\in\mathcal O_L\), the element

\[ \beta:=\alpha+Qw \]

lies in \(\mathcal O_L\). For every \(\sigma\in\Sigma\),

\[ |\sigma(\beta)| \le |\sigma(\alpha)|+Q|\sigma(w)| \le T+Q = R. \]

Thus

\[ \beta\in\mathcal C_L(R). \]

Therefore both

\[ x:=\sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{Q}\right) \]

and

\[ y:=\sigma_0\left(\frac{\beta}{Q}\right) \]

belong to \(P_L\). Moreover,

\[ y-x = \sigma_0(w), \]

and

\[ |\sigma_0(w)|=1. \]

So \((x,y)\) is an ordered unit-distance pair.

The map

\[ (\alpha,w) \mapsto \left( \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha}{Q}\right), \sigma_0\left(\frac{\alpha+Qw}{Q}\right) \right) \]

is injective. Indeed, the first coordinate determines \(\alpha\), and then the difference determines \(w\).

Hence the number of ordered unit-distance pairs in \(P_L\) is at least

\[ |\mathcal C_L(T)|\,|W_L|. \]

Since \(u(P_L)\) is half the number of ordered unit-distance pairs,

\[ u(P_L) \ge \frac12 |\mathcal C_L(T)|\,|W_L|. \]

Using the bounds above,

\[ u(P_L) \ge \frac12 a_0^r\mu^r = \frac12(a_0\mu)^r = \frac12 b_0^{(1+\eta)r}. \]

Since \(\delta=\eta/2\), for all sufficiently large \(r\),

\[ \frac12 b_0^{(1+\eta)r} \ge b_0^{(1+\delta)r}. \]

Thus, for all sufficiently large \(j\),

\[ u(P_{L_j}) \ge b_0^{(1+\delta)r_j}. \]

But

\[ |P_{L_j}|\le b_0^{r_j}, \]

so

\[ u(P_{L_j}) \ge |P_{L_j}|^{1+\delta}. \]

Discard finitely many initial fields and relabel the remaining point sets as

\[ P_1,P_2,\dots. \]

Then

\[ |P_i|\to\infty \]

and

\[ u(P_i)\ge |P_i|^{1+\delta} \]

for every \(i\). This proves the theorem. \(\square\)
